xAI Lawsuit Could Have Massive First Amendment Implications

How Does This Affect Me?

xAI is challenging Minnesota’s first-in-the-nation law targeting AI-generated “nudification” images, arguing that it violates the First Amendment. The case could help determine how far states can go in regulating AI-generated sexual content—and whether companies can be held responsible for how users wield their technology.

Minnesota passed a law restricting “AI nudification,” or the use of artificial intelligence to alter an existing image of a person to make it more sexually explicit than the original.

The law, HF 1606, forbids technology that allows a user, without specialized technical or artistic skill, to take an innocuous photo and alter it to show an “intimate part.” The law defines an intimate part as “the primary genital area, groin, nner thigh, buttocks, or breast.”

The first-of-its-kind law has been challenged in a lawsuit brought against the state by xAI. The Elon Musk-owned company argues that the definition of “intimate part” is overly broad and will prohibit images that are not sexually explicit at all. In its lawsuit, the company points to ordinary images, including one posted online by President Donald Trump, as examples of content that could potentially fall under the law.

Why did Minnesota Pass the law?

HF 1606 passed both chambers of the Minnesota legislature with near-unanimous support in late April and, by early May it was signed by the governor. Only one legislator voted against it. “To my knowledge, xAI is the only one of the major companies taking a stand in favor of nudification” said Jonathan Handel, a California-based entertainment and tech attorney.

At the bill’sfirst hearing in the Minnesota House’ Commerce, Finance and Policy committee on Feb. 19, three women testified that a man they knew had used AI to create nude images of them. The same man had nudified over 80 other women. The AI model he used was not specified.

“By simply clicking on a ‘nudify’ feature embedded in a platform, after uploading a non-explicit photo of any person at any time to the platform, this feature then allows anybody to generate an image that can portray a person as nude or in other sexual positions or ways,” said Minnesota state Rep. Jess Hanson, who authored HF 1606, during the hearing.

“Allowing apps to hide behind the lazy assertation of the first amendment is a moral surrender,” said Molly Kelly, one of the women who testified. “The software is the predator that weaponizes impulsivity; a twelve year old can snap a photo of a classmate and create a deepfake pornographic image in the time it takes to walk between classes.”

All three women said in their testimonies that they felts uncomfortable, unsafe and uneasy in public, bearing the knowledge that anyone could repeat what they had gone through previously.

Jessica Guistolise, another woman who testified, described the lasting consequences.

“I cannot overstate the harm this technology has caused. I was afraid to leave my house for weeks,” said Guistolise. “Because these images now live on the internet forever, my career is in jeopardy—not knowing where or when these images have been distributed… every single time I apply for a job, I will have to disclose this experience.”

Why is xAI Suing?

xAI filed its lawsuit against Minnestoa on July 27, just days before the law was scheduled to take effect.

The lawsuit bases its first amendment argument on the term “intimate part,” which they say is too broad and will make illegal normal, reasonable AI-generated content and will thus restrict first amendment rights.

“The Act restricts protected speech (i.e., the creation of images and videos) and thereby burdens the expressive rights of xAI and of people who use Grok Imagine,” the lawsuit says.

Grok, xAI’s most well-known generative AI model that allowed nudification, has been used to nudify women for purposes of both personal arousal and sexual harassment, and it allowed the making of extremely violent pornographic imagery.

Handel said xAI will likely argue that the images targeted by law do not necessarily constitute obscenity. “xAI is going to argue that this is not obscenity,” said Handel.

Additionally, the case could have implications on our understanding of free speech. While most AI-generated content should be covered by the first amendment according to Handel, who said “generative AI is an expressive tool,” the nudification of real people is likely to be considered obscenity, which is not constitutionally protected.

Legally, “obscenity” is generally defined by a three-prong test known as the Miller test. Obscene content must have or encourage excessive sexual interest, must be clearly offensive according to local or state laws and must lack artistic, literary, political or scientific value. If it falls short of any of these criteria, it is not considered obscene.

xAI’s argument, then, is that the HF 1606 will overly restrict non-obscene content: “Anyone with a computer and an internet connection can make humorous, scientific, artistic, satirical, or innocuous images that Minnesota’s HF 1606 bans as ‘nudification,’” says the lawsuit.

What has happened in court?

When xAI filed its lawsuit, it also asked the judge to temporarily block enforcement of HF 1606 while the case proceeded.

The first request to block enforcement was rejected by the judge. “xAI filed the motion on July 29, 2026, nearly three months after the law was signed, and only three days before the law is set to take effect. Such a delay in bringing the action and the motion suggests that harm is not immediate,” the judge said.

The judge presiding over the case heard arguments for a more permanent blocking of enforcement on August 19, which would prevent the law being enforced for the duration of the lawsuit. The judge denied the request to prevent enforcement of the law, a decision which has been appealed by xAI to the eighth circuit court.

Also on August 19, Minnesota state attorney general Keith Ellison filed a motion to dismiss the lawsuit. The hearing on the dismissal has been delayed, pending a decision on the aforementioned appeal of the more permanent enforcement blockage.

The federal government has since weighed in with a statement of interestby the Attorney General’s office, in which they seem to side with xAI, saying that a homogeneous set of expectations nationwide—as opposed to varied state rules and regulations—will permit the nation to continue advancing AI technologies. They also say that federal law—like the “TAKE IT DOWN act” and various child pornography laws—are already sufficient to prevent harm, making HF1606 redundant and unnecessary.

Early on with its image and video generation, Grok not only permitted the creation of sexual content, it was publicly touted to users, according to the aforementioned lawsuit.

At some point since then, xAI’s Grok (including the version of the chatbot integrated into the social media platform previously known as Twitter) began to refuse requests by users to nudify images.

Some of the legal opinions appear at odds with a recent ruling in Wisconsin, which threw out a charge on the grounds that AI-generated “virtual child pornography” can be protected under the first amendment in some circumstances.

Should xAI lose the lawsuit, “the outcome… would probably be seen as a part of a larger trend to start to hold social media companies responsible for the harm that they cause,” Handel said. “The involuntary nudification of people causes real harm.”
