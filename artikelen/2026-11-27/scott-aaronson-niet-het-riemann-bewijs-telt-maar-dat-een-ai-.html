Former OpenAI researcher says the Riemann proof matters less than the fact that an AI system can produce it

In a blog post, complexity theorist and former OpenAI researcher Scott Aaronson lays out what AI's recent math breakthroughs might mean for the rest of us.

Proving the Riemann hypothesis probably wouldn't change anyone's daily life, Aaronson writes. The ideas behind work like that could lead to new applications in science and engineering down the road, but it's anyone's guess which ones.

Aaronson, a professor at the University of Texas at Austin, argues the far bigger deal is that AI can do this kind of work at all. In his view, that could have "staggering implications for everyday life," from reshaping the global economy to threatening all life on Earth. "Opinions differ," he writes, but anyone serious about the topic should set aside the "'ooga booga, me only care about Riemann proof if it help me make sharper spear' caveman act."Ad

OpenAI's proofs show where faster algorithms hit hard limits

Aaronson walks through an OpenAI proof about matrix multiplication. The core question is how fast computational cost grows as matrices get larger. The new result, he writes, adds a matching lower bound to an existing fast algorithm. That shows how far computational cost can actually drop, at least in the model OpenAI studied.Ad

OpenAI got a similar result for the Fourier transform, a basic math tool behind audio, image, and signal processing. It found a faster way to compute it and also proved that it can't be done much faster. Proofs like that second one are called lower bounds, and Aaronson stresses them in particular. A new method only sets a record, but a lower bound shows where further improvements hit a hard limit.

The results have also changed Aaronson's outlook on the biggest open question in his field. That question, known as P versus NP, asks whether every problem whose answer computers can check quickly can also be solved quickly. Most researchers suspect the answer is no, but nobody has proved it. "I now feel in my bones, for the first time ever, that a proof of P ≠ NP is not far," Aaronson writes. Such a proof would confirm that some problems are easy for computers to check but hard to solve.

He adds that he's "virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources," that AI labs are sitting on "one or more bombshells" in cryptography, but says he doesn't have the details yet. Aaronson wrote in October that AI labs were testing whether their models could break cryptographic systems.Ad
