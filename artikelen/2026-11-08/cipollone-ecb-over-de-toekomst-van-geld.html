Shaping the future of money

But to do this, it must be trusted. It must allow people and businesses to transact safely and efficiently. And it must evolve with geopolitical and technological developments.

For centuries, central banks have issued money and safeguarded its value. That mandate remains unchanged. But the environment in which money is used is being transformed.

People are increasingly paying digitally. Tokenisation could reorganise the financial value chain. New solutions are being considered to enhance cross-border payments. And geopolitical fragmentation is making the resilience and governance of critical infrastructure ever more important.

Central banks must therefore ensure that the money they issue remains fit for the future.

This does not mean replacing the private sector or predicting which technology will prevail. It means providing a trusted public foundation for payments and finance on which private innovation can flourish. And it means preventing digitalisation from increasing fragmentation or creating new dependencies.

This is why the Eurosystem has adopted a comprehensive payments strategy.[1]

The strategy has four objectives: ensuring that central bank money can continue to settle retail and wholesale transactions as preferences and technologies evolve; strengthening Europe’s resilience and strategic autonomy; fostering an integrated, competitive and innovative payments ecosystem; and supporting the international role of the euro.

In other words, we are seeking to comprehensively upgrade money and payments in Europe.

The Western Balkans are also taking active policy steps to enhance and integrate payments in the region. Several countries, including Albania, have already joined the Single Euro Payments Area. And a clone of the Eurosystem’s fast payment system (TIPS), developed by Banca d’Italia, is already fully operational in Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro and will soon be launched in Albania, Kosovo and North Macedonia. It allows real-time, low-cost domestic transactions in local currencies around the clock, every day of the year.

This represents real, tangible progress that people will benefit from in their daily lives.

But we must always think one step ahead.

So today, I will discuss four key initiatives we are working on.

We are making it possible to settle tokenised asset transactions in central bank money and creating the conditions for an integrated and innovative tokenised finance ecosystem.

And we are interlinking fast payment systems to make cross-border payments faster and more efficient.

These initiatives have a common goal: ensuring access to safe public money while creating space for private innovation and international cooperation.

Modernising euro banknotes

Let me begin with the most familiar form of central bank money: cash.

In discussions about digitalisation, cash is sometimes talked about as a technology of the past. That is a mistake. In an age where cyber risks, hybrid threats and system outages make the headlines, cash remains the most resilient form of payment we have.

More than 31 billion euro banknotes, worth over €1.6 trillion, are in circulation. People do not just see cash as a means of payment; they also view it as a store of value and a safeguard in periods of uncertainty. And it is one of the most tangible symbols of European unity.

That is why Europe is not choosing between cash and digital payments. We are investing in both.

The Single Currency Package reflects this approach.[2] Alongside the proposed Regulation on the establishment of a digital euro, it includes a proposed Regulation on the legal tender status of euro banknotes and coins. The European Parliament and the Council of the European Union have adopted their respective positions, and the interinstitutional negotiations are under way.

But protecting cash is not enough. We must also prepare it for the future.

The next generation of euro banknotes will incorporate enhanced security features, have a smaller environmental impact and improve accessibility. We also want Europeans to feel that their banknotes reflect the cultural identity and natural environment they share.

This summer, almost ten million people shared their views in a public survey on the ten shortlisted design proposals. Two-thirds of the responses came from people under the age of 35.[3]

This remarkable level of participation shows that Europeans continue to care deeply about their banknotes.

The ECB’s Governing Council expects to decide on the final design concept around the end of this year. The future euro banknotes should enter circulation gradually from the early 2030s, while existing banknotes will remain valid.

So the future of public money will remain physical.

Preparing a future-ready digital euro

Today, central bank money is only available to the public as cash. But physical cash cannot be used online or for the ever-growing range of digital transactions.

Europe also lacks a digital solution that can be used for everyday payments across the euro area. Digital payments remain fragmented along national lines and use cases. And we have a strategic dependency: 13 euro area countries rely entirely on non-European companies for in-store card payments.
