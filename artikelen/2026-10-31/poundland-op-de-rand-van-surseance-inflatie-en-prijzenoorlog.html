Poundland in peril: how rampant inflation and price wars left £1 stores exposed

With the chain on the brink of administration, its low-profile US owners face questions about their conduct

‘It was a very successful and simple proposition,” laments one former Poundland boss. With every product in the shop sold at £1, and the price in its name, its proposition to shoppers was the clearest of any UK high street chain.

Now the UK’s first and biggest nationwide single-price retailer could disappear amid heavy competition and inflation that has eaten into its ability to offer bargains.

Its potential collapse has brought distress for its up to 12,000 staff in the run-up to Christmas, and as shoppers head to the chain for Halloween costumes and treats.

Meanwhile, its low-profile owners are facing questions from the prime minister over their behaviour in the week his government’s budget attempted to offer support to the high street.

Poundland’s future hangs in the balance – and not for the first time – after the 36-year-old retailer filed a notice of intention to appoint administrators at the high court on Thursday, with the advisory firm AlixPartners lined up to run the process.

The legal measure protects Poundland, which operates 600 stores, including Dealz outlets in Ireland, from the demands of creditors for 10 days while a solution to its problems is sought. However, the prospect of it avoiding mass store closures and job losses diminishes by the day as suppliers hold back on deliveries, hurting its ability to trade.

The chain’s potential demise comes after Gordon Brothers, the American restructuring specialist which bought Poundland for a nominal £1 from its previous owner in June last year, rejected a management-backed rescue bid for the whole chain by Martin Property Group.

While the Northern Irish firm is still in talks about taking on Poundland, it is understood its offer amounts to about £15m. That would pay off only half the £30m shareholder loan, the majority of which was taken on by Gordon Brothers when it bought the company. The deal would also include taking on a further, asset-backed loan worth about £90m, it is understood.

This deal has been rejected by Gordon Brothers despite Pepco, Poundland’s former owner, which holds the other £7.5m of the debt, being prepared to wait for repayment in the hope of saving the business, as first reported by Sky News.

One source who knows Poundland well said that Gordon Brothers had already taken about £10m in fees and interest out of the chain in the last year and that wanting another £30m was “egregious”. Gordon Brothers did not respond to a request for comment and has not confirmed this figure.

Gordon Brothers said earlier this week that options for the whole and parts of the business “are being thoroughly and objectively evaluated, with a view to minimising uncertainty for Poundland’s people and all relevant stakeholders”.

Sources close to the process said Gordon Brothers was unwilling to accept the Martin deal as the latest offer includes only about £7.5m up front and the rest over the next two years, leaving uncertainty for creditors, staff and suppliers.

One source said Gordon Brothers had sought bidders for the whole of Poundland but “the market had spoken” and only one little-known financial bidder with no history of running a large retail chain in the UK had come forward who might only go on to break up Poundland itself.

Instead, the restructuring specialist is understood to believe that suppliers and staff would be safer with the “trusted established retail operators” who have already put forward firm offers for parcels of outlets that amount to about half of Poundland’s stores, which could be converted to their formats.

A deal may yet be agreed with Martin over the next 10 days, especially with political pressure from Andy Burnham. On Friday, he told the Telegraph: “We want to see jobs protected. If there was a potential owner to avoid this that hasn’t been accepted, then the business secretary and I want to know why that is the case.”

For now, Gordon Brothers appears more keen on a break-up after losing faith in the likelihood of Poundland finding a route to long-term profitability amid tough times on the high street and heavy competition.

Rapid inflation on the cost of goods and labour, energy and business rates in recent years has forced Poundland to take a hit on profits and sell products for more than £1, including items for £5 or more.

In the past, Poundland removed products if it could not sell them profitably for a pound, but its choices have become more limited. High inflation – triggered first by Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine and then the Middle East conflict – has exposed a business model which always appeared to have a shelf life.

In most supermarkets a two-litre bottle of milk costs over £2, a pack of KitKats is at least £1.50 and a tin of Heinz baked beans is about £1.40, according to the price comparison site Trolley. Cutting the size of Poundland has, meanwhile, reduced its scale, making it harder to get good deals from suppliers to protect margins.

Poundland has also admitted that it made mistakes in recent years, including putting its Pepco clothing ranges into stores, which proved unpopular with UK shoppers.

The expansion of cut-price chains such as Savers, The Range, B&M, The Works and Home Bargains alongside budget online rivals such as Temu, Shein and Amazon means the cheaper end of the UK retail market is now very crowded.

Meanwhile, the low-wage shoppers that make up the core of Poundland’s clientele have little spare cash for non-essentials amid rising household bills. Those with more to spare have more options in their supermarket shop, where they are increasingly locked in by loyalty-card-linked discounts.

Poundland’s pre-tax losses widened to £85.3m in the year to September 2025, from a loss of £45.4m in 2024, as sales at its outlets in the UK, Republic of Ireland and Isle of Man slid almost 12% to £1.55bn, according to accounts filed at Companies House.

In September Poundland said its revenues had returned to growth, up 3.3% at established stores in the latest three-month period, but sources said the business was not generating enough cash to pay off its loans.

US-headquartered Gordon Brothers has a long history of involvement in selling off the assets of UK high street businesses, including offloading the stock and and fittings of failed retailers such as Toys R Us.

It previously owned the British fashion brands Laura Ashley and Ben Sherman, where it oversaw the closure of their stores, and it recently bought LK Bennett, which has also closed its stores this year.

One advisory expert said Poundland’s owners had intended the buyout to be a rescue operation rather than a break-up but ultimately: “Gordon Brothers has ended up doing what Gordon Brothers do. Leopards do not change their spots.”
