AI adoption in Europe

My aim today is to describe how AI is reshaping the euro area economy.[1] Although the development of frontier AI models is primarily taking place in the United States and China, the adoption of AI will be a key determinant of European productivity growth in the coming years, while expenditure on AI adoption also currently constitutes a significant stimulus to the euro area economy.[2] In addition, European firms are part of the global AI supply chain, while European investors are contributing to the funding of the global buildout of AI infrastructure through equity and debt markets and the banking system.

I will also describe the key patterns we see in the data, with a primary focus on how the surveys conducted by the ECB can provide insights into the AI adoption process and the implications for the financial system.[3][4]

Aggregate trends

Digital investment in the euro area has expanded by 73 per cent in real terms since 2014, increasing from 2.3 per cent to 3.3 per cent of GDP (Chart 1a). While digital investment has grown far more quickly in the United States, the surge in digital investment (with an estimated clear acceleration since 2024) marks a distinct shift in the structure of the euro area economy.[5] As shown in Chart 1b, in the euro area digital investment has primarily been in intangible assets, while investment in data centres has constituted a relatively small share.[6]

In the ECB Corporate Telephone Survey, firms report that AI has added about 10 per cent to their capital expenditure plans this year. AI-related demand has helped activity to hold up over the last two years – as reflected by the contributions of the “computers and other electronics” and “information and communication services” sectors to total market production (Chart 2a). As illustrated in Chart 2b, Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) indicators show that output, employment and new orders have developed considerably more favourably in the technology equipment subsector than in other manufacturing subsectors. For services, the software subsector has performed better than other services sectors in terms of output, albeit not in terms of employment and new orders.

AI has been the main driver of global goods trade over the last year (Chart 3a). European firms are contributors to the global AI supply chain. Table 4 shows that AI-related exports and imports are growing quickly in the euro area, especially in relation to core data centre goods, which contain directly deployed hardware components related to AI compute, storage and networking capacity. Possibly related to differences in composition, the increase in AI import prices in the euro area is more muted than for the other major economic regions (Chart 3b). Chart 5 shows that, while AI-related price increases have contributed materially to the upward shift in goods inflation, the impact on headline inflation is limited owing to the relatively low weight of AI-exposed goods in the consumer price index (13.3 per cent in the non-energy industrial goods subcomponent and 3.4 per cent of the HICP).

AI intensity groups are based on OECD methodology (for details, see “A sectoral taxonomy of AI intensity”, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers, No 30, December 2024). The classification comprises three AI intensity groups: high, medium and low, based on sectoral quartile allocations across four indicators: 1. AI human capital; 2. AI innovation; 3. Barrier-adjusted AI exposure; and 4. AI use. A sector is classified as high if it ranks in the top quartile for at least two indicators and not in the bottom quartile for the remaining two, and as low if it ranks in the bottom quartile for at least two indicators and not in the top quartile for the remaining two. Sectors such as IT services, media, telecommunications, finance and scientific research and development are classified as high-intensity, while food products, textiles, wood and paper, construction and hospitality are low-intensity. Sectors falling outside these criteria are classified as medium AI-intensive. The latest observations are for 20 October 2026.

US firms (especially the hyperscalers building out the AI infrastructure) have been large-scale issuers of euro-denominated bonds (reverse Yankees). So far, the available data show that purchases by European investors of euro-denominated bonds issued by US firms have increased alongside a decline in their purchases of US Treasury bonds (Chart 9a).[8]

The AI boom has also led to large initial public offerings (IPOs), including the SpaceX IPO in the second quarter of 2026. In this context, euro area investors have stepped up their acquisitions of US equities, and at the same time they have lowered their purchases of other foreign equities (Chart 9b). Overall, these developments, together with analysis of credit spreads and the book-cover ratios of euro area corporate issuers and credit spreads, suggest only limited crowding-out up to now.[9]
