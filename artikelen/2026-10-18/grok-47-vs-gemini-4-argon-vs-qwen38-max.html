Grok 4.7 vs Gemini 4 Argon vs Qwen3.8-Max: the quick verdict

Gemini 4 Argon currently holds the strongest published agentic and software-engineering scores of the three. On the APEX-Agents benchmark it scores 82.2%, against 54.6% for Grok 4.7, a 27.6-point gap that holds up as the single largest documented spread between any two of these models on a shared test. On the Respan Index, Argon posts 71.4 (good for the number-two overall spot on that board) versus 49.7 for Grok 4.7. Qwen3.8-Max doesn’t appear on either of those two boards in the sources reviewed here, but its own benchmark suite shows the September refresh roughly doubling scores on two coding tests: TerminalBench 3.0 jumped from 11.3 to 29.0, and ProgramBench Almost Solved rose from 10.5 to 28.0.

Pricing tells a different story. Grok 4.7 and Qwen3.8-Max both list output at $6 per million tokens, while Gemini 4 Argon’s introductory output rate is $10 per million tokens through November 30, 2026, climbing to $20 after. Context window favors Argon and Qwen3.8-Max almost equally at roughly 1 million tokens each, while Grok 4.7 caps out at 500,000 tokens, half the other two.

Release timeline: three launches in four weeks

The compressed release schedule is itself part of the story. Alibaba Cloud opened global API access to Qwen3.8-Max through its Model Studio platform on August 3, 2026, then shipped a substantial refresh under the “0902” tag on September 2, 2026, improving all eight of its published coding benchmarks in one update. xAI followed with Grok 4.7 on September 21, 2026, continuing the cadence it set with Grok 4.5 and Grok 4.6 earlier in the year. Google announced Gemini 4 Argon on September 30, 2026, initially in limited release, before taking it to full general availability across the Gemini app, Google Cloud, and the Gemini API on October 13, 2026.

That sequencing matters for anyone benchmarking these models today. Qwen3.8-Max has had the longest runway in production, which is reflected in Alibaba Cloud Model Studio’s customer base growing eightfold year over year as of March 2026, according to Alibaba’s own cloud revenue disclosures. Gemini 4 Argon, by contrast, has been broadly available for less than a week as of this writing, so real-world usage data and third-party benchmark coverage are still catching up to the model’s published scores.

Benchmark performance: where each model actually wins

Benchmark results for these three models split cleanly along two lines: agentic, long-horizon software engineering versus raw coding throughput on narrower tasks. Gemini 4 Argon’s strongest documented results come from benchmarks built around multi-step, tool-using work. Google reports Argon hitting 88.8% on LABBench 2 and 76.0% on RiemannBench, alongside the 77.9% DeepSWE v1.1 score the company calls state-of-the-art for long-horizon software engineering. Those numbers line up with how Google is positioning the model: for complex, multi-file coding tasks, enterprise workflows, and cybersecurity research rather than short Q&A exchanges.

Grok 4.7’s published scores sit well behind Argon on the agentic boards but land respectably on more traditional coding tests, with a 46.3% result on CursorBench 4.0, according to DataCamp’s breakdown of the Grok 4.7 launch. That score matters because CursorBench is built from real developer edits inside the Cursor IDE rather than a synthetic benchmark, and Grok’s lineage (both 4.5 and 4.6 were trained in part on Cursor’s production coding data) shows up directly in that result. The gap between Grok’s CursorBench score and its 54.6% APEX-Agents score suggests the model holds up better on single-session coding tasks than on long-running autonomous agent work, which is the opposite emphasis from Argon.

Qwen3.8-Max’s benchmark story is about trajectory rather than a single headline number. Alibaba’s own disclosure for the September 2 refresh shows TerminalBench 3.0 rising from 11.3 to 29.0 and ProgramBench Almost Solved climbing from 10.5 to 28.0, both more than doubling in a single update cycle. Alibaba states that all eight of the coding benchmarks it tracks internally improved with the 0902 snapshot, covering long-horizon autonomous development, agent collaboration, and visual analysis. Because Qwen3.8-Max doesn’t report scores on the same third-party boards as Argon and Grok, a precise head-to-head ranking across all three isn’t possible from public data, and this piece avoids forcing one.

Pricing breakdown: cost per million tokens

*Estimated using a simplified 1,000-token input / 1,000-token output request pattern with no caching, for illustration only; actual workload costs depend heavily on prompt length, caching hit rate, and reasoning/thinking token consumption, which several reviewers flag as a hidden cost driver for Grok 4.7 specifically. VentureBeat’s coverage of the Grok 4.7 launch noted that the model pairs real coding gains with the same affordable headline pricing as its predecessor, but warned that high token consumption on harder tasks threatens the real-world return on investment that the sticker price implies.

The practical upshot: Grok 4.7 and Qwen3.8-Max are priced identically at the list-price level, both undercutting Gemini 4 Argon’s introductory output rate by 40% and its post-November rate by 70%. Argon’s much deeper cache discount, at $0.10 per million tokens during the introductory window versus $0.50 for Grok and roughly $0.25 for Qwen, partially offsets that gap for workloads with high prompt reuse, such as repeated analysis over the same codebase or document set. Teams running one-shot queries without caching will feel Argon’s higher output price directly; teams running agentic loops that re-read the same context repeatedly will see the gap narrow.

Context window and output limits

Context window is the cleanest differentiator of the three. Gemini 4 Argon and Qwen3.8-Max both land near the 1-million-token mark (1,048,576 and 1,000,000 tokens respectively), while Grok 4.7 caps at 500,000 tokens, exactly half. For most day-to-day coding and chat tasks that difference won’t matter, since the vast majority of prompts never approach even Grok’s lower ceiling. It becomes material for two specific use cases: ingesting entire large codebases or long document sets in a single call, and long-running agent sessions that accumulate tool outputs, file reads, and intermediate reasoning across dozens of steps without summarization.

Google’s documentation for Argon describes its 1,048,576-token limit as unified across input, output, “thinking,” and tool or function calls combined, rather than input and output being tracked separately. That’s a meaningful architectural detail: a long agentic session that burns through reasoning tokens on Argon eats into the same budget that governs how much it can read and write, whereas a model with separate input and output ceilings handles that differently. Developers building long-horizon agents on Argon should budget for thinking-token consumption explicitly rather than assuming the full million tokens is available for file content alone.

Speed and latency

Throughput data is thinner than pricing or benchmark data across all three models, but the figures that are published favor Argon. OpenRouter’s aggregated routing data shows Gemini 4 Argon generating a median 77.5 tokens per second across providers, against 66.0 tokens per second for Grok 4.7, roughly a 17% speed advantage for Argon. No comparable median throughput figure has been published for Qwen3.8-Max in the sources reviewed for this piece, so it isn’t included in the speed comparison; claims of relative speed improvements for any of these models should be treated as directional rather than as controlled, apples-to-apples measurements until a shared benchmark publishes all three side by side.

Grok 4.7 vs Gemini 4 Argon: the agentic benchmark gap explained

The single most striking number in this comparison is the 27.6-point gap between Gemini 4 Argon’s 82.2% APEX-Agents score and Grok 4.7’s 54.6%. That’s not a rounding difference; it’s the kind of spread that typically separates models built for distinctly different workloads rather than two frontier releases from the same generation. APEX-Agents is designed to measure multi-step, tool-using autonomous behavior, which lines up with how Google is explicitly marketing Argon, for long-running software engineering and enterprise workflows rather than quick single-turn answers.

Grok 4.7’s relative strength on CursorBench 4.0 (46.3%) versus its weaker APEX-Agents showing suggests the model is tuned more for the kind of short, high-frequency coding interactions a developer has inside an editor, line-by-line edits, inline suggestions, quick refactors, rather than for agents that run unsupervised for dozens of steps. Teams building autonomous coding agents that operate with minimal human oversight should weight the APEX-Agents and DeepSWE results heavily; teams building copilot-style assistance embedded in a developer’s existing workflow should weight CursorBench and raw latency more heavily.

What the pricing changes mean for budgeting in Canada

Gemini 4 Argon’s scheduled price increase on December 1, 2026, doubling both input and output rates, deserves specific attention from Canadian teams budgeting API spend into next year. A workload costing roughly $12 per 1,000 average requests today under Argon’s introductory pricing will cost closer to $24 under the same usage pattern once the standard rate kicks in, a 100% increase with no corresponding benchmark change announced alongside it. Teams currently prototyping on Argon’s introductory rate should model their production costs against the post-December pricing, not the launch-window rate, before committing budget for a 2027 rollout.

Grok 4.7 and Qwen3.8-Max don’t carry a similar announced step-up as of this writing, which makes their $2/$6 per million token pricing a more stable baseline for multi-quarter budget planning, assuming no change is announced before the next model refresh from either vendor. Given that all three companies have shipped new point releases every four to eight weeks through 2026, any pricing assumption built today should be revisited at the next release cycle regardless of which model a team chooses.

Multimodal and feature differences

Google positions Gemini 4 Argon as multimodal across coding, finance, legal, cybersecurity, and scientific research tasks, with configurable “thinking” levels (low, medium, high) that let developers trade latency and cost against reasoning depth on a per-request basis. Qwen3.8-Max-0902 similarly supports multimodal inputs alongside long-context processing, structured outputs, and agentic workflows, with Alibaba specifically citing improved visual analysis as one of the gains in the September refresh. Grok 4.7’s documentation in the sources reviewed for this piece focuses primarily on text and code; a detailed, verified multimodal capability matrix for Grok 4.7 was not available at the time of writing, so this piece does not claim parity or gaps in that area beyond what xAI has published.

Availability also differs meaningfully. Gemini 4 Argon reaches the widest footprint out of the gate, shipping simultaneously across the consumer Gemini app, the Gemini API, Google Cloud, and Vertex AI. Qwen3.8-Max is most deeply integrated into Alibaba’s own cloud ecosystem through Model Studio and QwenWork, which is the more natural fit for organizations already running infrastructure on Alibaba Cloud. Grok 4.7’s distribution leans heavily on developer tools, specifically Cursor, Grok Build, and the SpaceXAI console, positioning it closer to a coding-tool embed than a general-purpose consumer or enterprise platform play.

Independent benchmark caveats worth knowing

Every benchmark cited in this comparison comes with a caveat that matters for anyone making a procurement decision off these numbers alone. First, Qwen3.8-Max does not report on the same third-party boards (Respan Index, APEX-Agents) that Argon and Grok 4.7 both appear on, so any ranking that places all three on a single scale for those specific tests is not possible from public data. Second, Grok 4.7’s Terminal-Bench 4.0 score varies by source, 16.2% versus 37.6%, likely reflecting different test harnesses, effort settings, or tool access configurations rather than a single canonical number; this piece defaults to the more conservative figure throughout. Third, all three vendors’ headline Intelligence Index-style scores (Artificial Analysis for Grok, internal indices for the others) are run under vendor-influenced or third-party conditions that can shift meaningfully with prompt engineering, effort level, and tool access, so a 2-3 point gap on any single index shouldn’t be treated as a durable, universal ranking.

The more durable signal across all three launches is architectural and pricing-based rather than purely benchmark-based: Argon’s million-token unified context and higher price point, Grok 4.7’s Cursor-centric distribution and smaller context ceiling, and Qwen3.8-Max’s balance of competitive pricing with Alibaba’s enterprise packaging. Those structural differences are less likely to shift with the next incremental benchmark run than a 2-point score gap is.

Enterprise strategy: three different go-to-market plays

Behind the benchmark numbers, each company is running a visibly different playbook for how it wants this model adopted. Google’s approach with Gemini 4 Argon is breadth-first: the model shipped simultaneously into the consumer-facing Gemini app, the developer-facing Gemini API, and the enterprise-facing Google Cloud and Vertex AI surfaces, all on the same day. That lets a solo developer, a startup wiring up an API key, and a bank’s procurement team evaluating Vertex AI all reach the same model through the channel that fits them, without waiting for a staged rollout.
