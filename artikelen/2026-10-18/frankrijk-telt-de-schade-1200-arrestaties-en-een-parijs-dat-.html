Arrests over France protests rise to 1,800, with whole streets in Paris ‘devastated’ – Europe live

Why are the protests happening?

If you’re just catching up on the demonstrations across Europe which turned violent on Saturday, this is why some of them are happening.

Why are people taking to the streets across Europe?

People have been protesting in countries across Europe, including Spain, France and Portugal over their unhappiness at the state of schools and wider cost of living crisis.

In France, the activism has largely been led by high school pupils who have clashed with police in often violent protests over long hours, missing teachers, dilapidated classrooms and an opaque university admissions system. Pupils routinely miss months of teaching due to unfilled staff absences.

Other demonstrators have then joined student marches to voice their unhappiness about rising living costs, including over fuel prices after US bombing caused the effective closure of the strait of Hormuz.

The General Confederation of Labour (CGT), one of France’s largest unions, has backed the protests and called for lower prices, higher pensions and boosted wages.

French president Emmanual Macron is nearing the end of his time in the Élysée Palace after serving two terms.

Over the Pyrenees in Spain activists took to the streets in September over housing prices.

They were triggered by the eviction of a 87-year-old woman from her home after an investment fund hiked her rent by 230%. María del Carmen Abascal died a fortnight later after being admitted to hospital.

Protesters have called for lower rents, an end to evictions and a ban on investment funds buying property.

Similar pleas were made across the Iberian border in Portugal on Saturday, with hundreds gathering in central Lisbon.

Portuguese housing prices rose by 124% between 2015 and 2024, roughly twice as fast as the EU average, according to official data.

Squeezed household budgets were also at the centre of marches in Milan, Italy.

A march on Saturday began peacefully but ended with masked rioters throwing stones, paving blocks and other objects at riot police and setting fire to barricades made from bar tables.

Meanwhile student-protests in Albania against the prime minister, Edi Rama, entered their sixth day on Sunday.

Demonstrators have said they are unhappy with perceived corruption, a lack of jobs and poor education standards.

The education protests in France began in late September, week after the start of the school year.

However demonstrations have since intensified, reaching the high point on Saturday.

Saturday was also the first day of cost of living protests in Italy, Portugal and Belgium.

In Albania protests have been taking place since the summer, triggered by a luxury coastal development backed by Donald Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner.

In France the government has said it would deploy 3,000 more substitute teachers to deal with shortages, and said it would include students in decision-making for schools.

It also scrapped plans to increase some university registration fees, which it had announced for next year’s budget.

Fuel protests in mid-September led to an announcement by the Elysee that the government would increase petrol subsidies for certain industries.

Spain’s president Pedro Sánchez attempted to pass emergency housing legislation that would have protected tenants in the rental market.

The Socialist party leader’s defeat led him to calling an early election for 29 November, as he told the public he needed a “broader progressive majority” to get the new laws through.

Macron will give a televised address on Sunday night after holding a crisis meeting with key ministers.

His interior minister Laurent Nunez appeared to signal the French government are not willing to concede further, as he blamed “far left” protesters and “criminals who looted and ransacked” buildings across France.

The country will go to the polls in April 2027.
