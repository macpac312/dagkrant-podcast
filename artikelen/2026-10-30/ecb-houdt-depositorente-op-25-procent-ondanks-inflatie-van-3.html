Monetary policy statement (with Q&A)

Christine Lagarde, President of the ECB, Boris Vujčić, Vice-President of the ECB

Good afternoon, the Vice-President and I welcome you to our press conference.

The Governing Council today decided to keep the three key ECB interest rates unchanged. Energy prices have continued to rise, posing upside risks to the inflation outlook and downside risks to the growth outlook. While the full effects of the energy shock are still unfolding, underlying inflation has remained broadly stable so far and wages do not show a material response at this stage. The increased magnitude and duration of the energy shock, as well as the implications for indirect and second-round effects, are likely to drive up inflation and lower growth. Financing conditions have tightened since our last meeting, which is likely to weigh on inflation and growth. We are committed to setting monetary policy to ensure that inflation stabilises at our two per cent target in the medium term.

We remain well positioned to navigate the current elevated uncertainty. We will follow a data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting approach to determining the appropriate monetary policy stance. In particular, our interest rate decisions will be based on our assessment of the inflation outlook and the risks surrounding it, in light of the incoming economic and financial data, as well as the dynamics of underlying inflation and the strength of monetary policy transmission. We are not pre-committing to a particular rate path.

The decisions taken today are set out in a press release available on our website.

I will now outline in more detail how we see the economy and inflation developing and will then explain our assessment of financial and monetary conditions.

Economic activity

The incoming data suggest that the euro area economy continued to expand in the third quarter of the year. Services have recovered, after weakening at the start of the Middle East conflict. Manufacturing, digital services and exports are benefiting from AI-related business investment. Government spending on infrastructure and defence is also supporting activity. These factors should continue to underpin growth in the near term. At the same time, renewed energy price increases have hit consumer confidence, and higher financing costs are likely to weigh on growth.

The unemployment rate remained at 6.4 per cent in August. Growth in employment and the labour force remains subdued, while productivity has gradually picked up.

Over the medium term, a recovery in confidence and real incomes, driven by gradually declining energy prices and a robust labour market, should bolster domestic demand. Investment in AI-related activities and government spending on infrastructure and defence should also continue to support growth. Export growth should pick up on the back of strengthening foreign demand.

Higher potential growth requires structural reform and has to be underpinned by sound public finances. Fiscal sustainability is a crucial anchor for broader economic stability. Fiscal responses to the energy shock should be temporary, targeted and tailored. Simplifying and harmonising rules across the EU’s Single Market, accelerating the energy transition and completing the savings and investments union are key building blocks to make our economy more dynamic and resilient. As the process for agreeing on the legal framework for the digital euro moves into its final stage, we reiterate the importance of reaching agreement on the Single Currency Package as quickly as possible.

Inflation

Inflation rose to 3.8 per cent in September, from 3.2 per cent in August. Energy price inflation increased markedly, to 18.9 per cent, from 14.3 per cent in August. Diesel and petrol prices reached new peaks in September, in part reflecting elevated refining margins. Inflation excluding energy increased to 2.3 per cent in September, from 2.1 per cent in August. Food price inflation increased to 1.3 per cent, from 1.1 per cent. Services inflation increased to 3.2 per cent in September, from 3.0 per cent in August, while goods inflation declined to 1.1 per cent from 1.2 per cent.

Indicators of underlying inflation have remained broadly stable, although the early stages of the pricing chain point to upward pressures. Wage pressures remain moderate. Compensation per employee grew by 3.3 per cent in the second quarter, down from 3.6 per cent in the first quarter, while rising productivity has helped contain the growth of unit labour costs. Our forward-looking wage tracker suggests that negotiated wage growth will fluctuate between 2.5 per cent and 2.8 per cent in the remaining months of 2026 and average 2.6 per cent in the first three quarters of 2027. Inflation expectations over shorter horizons have risen, but most measures of longer-term inflation expectations stand at around 2 per cent, supporting the stabilisation of inflation around target in the medium term.

Energy prices have increased beyond the baseline path foreseen in the September ECB staff projections. This is likely to raise inflation further in the near term and keep headline inflation well above target over the coming quarters. Thereafter, declining energy inflation, together with the effects of higher interest rates, should support the return of inflation to target.

Risk assessment

The risks to the growth outlook are to the downside. This is due, in particular, to the Middle East conflict and recent developments in Russia’s unjustified war against Ukraine. Continued disruption of energy supplies could cause energy prices to rise further and for longer than currently expected. This would weigh on real incomes, spending and investment. A worsening of global financial market sentiment or intensifying spillovers in global bond markets could tighten financing conditions and thereby dampen demand. Fiscal uncertainty may also further tighten financing conditions and weaken confidence, thereby lowering consumption and investment. A resurgence of trade tensions between major economies could further disrupt supply chains, reduce exports and weaken consumption and investment. By contrast, growth could turn out to be higher if the economy and energy markets were to adapt more quickly than expected to the disruption caused by the ongoing conflicts or if these were resolved sustainably. Moreover, the adoption of new technologies by euro area firms and government spending on infrastructure and defence, as well as reforms to enhance productivity and complete the EU’s Single Market, may drive up growth by more than expected.

The risks to the inflation outlook are to the upside. This is due, in particular, to the Middle East conflict and recent developments in Russia’s unjustified war against Ukraine. The energy shock could intensify further and its effects on other prices and wages could be stronger than currently expected. Gas prices, in particular, could increase if supply is disrupted further or an unusually cold winter coincides with low storage levels. The longer energy prices stay high, the more likely they are to drive up broader inflation through indirect and second-round effects. Renewed trade tensions could give rise to more fragmented global supply chains, curtail the supply of critical raw materials and worsen capacity constraints in the euro area economy. Extreme weather events, potentially reinforced by intensifying “El Niño” conditions, and the unfolding climate and nature crises more broadly, could drive up food prices by more than expected. By contrast, inflation could turn out to be lower if ongoing geopolitical conflicts were resolved sustainably or if indirect or second-round effects from higher energy prices proved less pronounced than anticipated. More volatile and risk-averse financial markets, together with higher long-term interest rates, could weigh on demand and thereby lower inflation as well.

Financial and monetary conditions

Financial conditions have tightened since our previous meeting. Interest rates have broadly moved up, while stock prices have fallen.

The cost of market-based debt for firms rose to 4.1 per cent in August, from 4.0 per cent in July, while bank lending rates for firms remained at 3.8 per cent. Growth in bank lending to firms slowed to an annual rate of 4.2 per cent in August and September, from 4.4 per cent in July, while corporate bond issuance grew at an annual rate of 4.2 per cent in September, up from 3.8 per cent in August and 3.5 per cent in July. Credit standards for loans to firms tightened in the third quarter, as reported in our latest bank lending survey, with banks citing concern about the economic risks faced by their customers. Despite higher financing needs for inventories and working capital, corporate loan demand declined, reflecting in particular higher interest rates.

Mortgage rates rose to 3.6 per cent in August, from 3.5 per cent in July, while mortgage lending growth edged down to 3.0 per cent in September. Credit standards for mortgage lending were unchanged in the third quarter, whereas demand for mortgages declined substantially given higher interest rates.

Conclusion

The Governing Council today decided to keep the three key ECB interest rates unchanged. We are committed to setting monetary policy to ensure that inflation stabilises at our two per cent target in the medium term. We will follow a data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting approach to determining the appropriate monetary policy stance. Our interest rate decisions will be based on our assessment of the inflation outlook and the risks surrounding it, in light of the incoming economic and financial data, as well as the dynamics of underlying inflation and the strength of monetary policy transmission. We are not pre-committing to a particular rate path.

In any case, we stand ready to adjust all of our instruments within our mandate to ensure that inflation stabilises sustainably at our medium-term target and to preserve the smooth functioning of monetary policy transmission.

We are now ready to take your questions.

The first one, on monetary policy, is that for the first time in I don't know how long the mentioning of second-round effects in your press release was quite striking. So does it also mean that the market is about right to expect a rate hike in December, because clearly there is now a risk of second-round effects?

My second question is: How do you marry your position which was, you said, I think it was spring this year, that the captain stays on the ship when there are clouds on the horizon, given that the euro area is probably in a worse situation than in spring, looking at France, looking at the inflation picture, and perhaps you can tell us more about your motivation?

I would suggest that we deal first and foremost with the monetary policy statement and the monetary policy decision questions, and that if there are additional questions in relation to me, for instance, that we keep that for the end, because the real order of business here is monetary policy. So your second question, I'll deal with it later in the press conference, if that's okay with you.

You are right that second-round effects – and the risk of second-round effects – is something that we have paid very special attention to. I think I said that in previous press conferences. As inflation rises, as the risk to inflation has increased, we pay special attention to the direct effect – we see it clearly – to the indirect effect – we have seen an increase of those indirect effects – and to the secondary effects, which would require a particular policy decision.

We are very attentive and, at this point in time, we are not seeing a risk of second-round effects in addition to what is already included in the projections. So if you will, the projections that were prepared in September did include the risk of second-round effects. So we are looking very attentively to that and, for the moment, as I think we say very clearly in the monetary policy statement, we do not see a material risk of second-round effects at the moment.

But give me the opportunity, because I deferred your second question to a bit later, to give you a little bit of flavour of the debate that we had. Because while we were very attentive to second-round effects, and in particular to the evolution of wages, to the wage tracker, to the compensation per employee projections, to every instrument that we have to measure wages in all sectors, including the opening position of unions in the coming collective bargaining agreement discussions, we have also looked at what has really significantly changed compared to last September.

And the two key changes that we have seen is, first and foremost, the energy prices. And on that front, it's a combination of oil prices, but more importantly, petrol, fuel, gas, and of course refining margins. And that has increased significantly, it's well reflected in the energy inflation numbers – 18.9[1] if I recall, as compared to a lower number in August[2] – so a clear increase there.

But another new development that we have observed as well since September is the cost of long-term financing, which has indeed increased. And of course, we look at numbers. And the average 10-year sovereign yields have risen by around 35 basis points since our last cut-off for the September projections.
