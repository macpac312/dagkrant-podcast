Gukesh Survives Pressure To Hold Sindarov To Draw In World Championship Game 1

GM Javokhir Sindarov seemed to get the better of a tense opening battle in game one of the Salesforce FIDE World Championship 2026, but World Champion Gukesh Dommaraju survived pressure on the board and clock to make a draw. Sindarov, who faced the Berlin Endgame in his first ever world championship game, was even penalized two minutes for incorrectly claiming a draw at the end—no harm was done, as the players shook hands moments later with the score level at 0.5-0.5.

Game two, when Gukesh has the white pieces, starts Wednesday, November 25, at 9 a.m. ET / 15:00 CET / 7:30 p.m. IST.

Nothing in chess is quite as fraught with nerves as a world championship match, even for those with experience, though sometimes the first game can also be a relief. Gukesh commented in the post-game press conference:

The easiest thing is actually to go and play the game. There’s so much thinking, so much work to do before it, but once you get to the game it’s like you’re home and it’s a game you’ve been playing for a lot of years—it feels safe! Otherwise, all the nerves and all the hype is a little tougher to handle.

The venue was smaller than we've been used to for title matches, the Fondation Martin Bodmer on the shores of Lake Geneva, with the silence of the library interrupted by chess fans filtering in for their agreed time slots on the balcony.

Both players noted there was some noise of creaking floors, but also welcomed chess fans being able to get up close to the action. Sindarov added, "The good thing is it's nice to have more people in the playing hall—otherwise it’d be boring with just one board!"

There were plenty of people at the start, including FIDE President Timur Turlov and his deputy, the five-time World Champion Viswanathan Anand. The dignitaries played 1.e4 and 1...e5, before the players did the same.

Gukesh pulled a small surprise by playing the most infamously solid opening with Black, the Berlin Defense, but it was perhaps more unexpected that Sindarov, for the first time in his career, played 6.Bxc6 and went for the Berlin Endgame, which GM Vladimir Kramnik used to defuse and then dethrone GM Garry Kasparov in the 2000 World Championship match in London.

Normally, the first game you try to play more solid and adapt to the tournament hall and everything. I’m trying to join the tournament and not trying to push too much and trying to keep control of the game, so I think it’s a nice choice—and a really high quality game.

The opening followed a game Gukesh had played against GM Alireza Firouzja in the 2024 Sinquefield Cup, shortly before he won the world title, but with the twist that Gukesh had White in that game. He called it "a pretty good game," before adding, "but OK, but I don’t know how much of a role it played in this game."

Nevertheless, when Sindarov varied with 12.Be3 compared to 12.b3, Gukesh sank into his first prolonged think of the game. Polgar looked on with concern, suggesting that the Berlin Endgame might not suit Gukesh's style, just as it proved frustrating for Kasparov.

The danger that Polgar identified seemed to grow as Sindarov's next move, the rare 13.Ne4, sent Gukesh into an even longer think.

The world champion later explained, "I saw it was possible, but maybe I underestimated it a little bit. The point I guess is if 13...Bf5 then 14.Ng3, sac the pawn on c2 (14...Bxc2), and he gets the f5-square."

While Gukesh contemplated if he should go for that—it seems you can, but it's not something you want to be working out over-the-board—Sindarov was able to relax in his rest area. It was Ding chilling taken one step further!

Sindarov explained what he was doing: "When I’m somewhere, I’m always thinking about the game and trying not to calculate, but just understand which moves he has to play."

Gukesh ultimately chose the modest 13...Bd7, which had the virtue of finally getting Sindarov to pause. In fact, the challenger commented, "I was thinking it’s not a logical move to play." From there, both sides were engaged in a tense battle, with Sindarov unsure of his decision to push 14.c4!? (his first instinct was the natural 14.Rd2), while a few moves later Gukesh invested a huge 36 minutes in the position after 17.b3.

It was potentially treacherous for Black, with the attempt to target the e5-pawn with 17...Ra5? all but losing to 18.Bg5!.

He had to be feeling his chances were growing with every minute, but Gukesh was happy with his "quite precise" and "very clever" 17...Bb4 18.Re2 (Sindarov regretted not trying 18.Rb2) 18...h6!, and then was feeling good when the white knight dropped back to g3 and he was able to play 19...c5, stopping the other knight coming to d4 once and for all.

The one issue, as you can see, remained the clock, with Gukesh 40 minutes down and with only 16 minutes for his next 21 moves—a key feature of the time control in Geneva is that although the players have a generous two hours for their first 40 moves they get no additional time each move until move 41.

The players were impacted by the time situation, with Sindarov noting, "I'm trying to push at every moment because of the clock." Gukesh felt he'd done the hard work, however, explaining, "I was happy that I managed to spend some time and find a good solution, and then the moves after that were pretty easy."

The position flared into life, with 27.Nxf5+! an attractive move by Sindarov.
