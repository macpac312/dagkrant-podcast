Looking beyond the baseline: how scenario synthesis helps assess risks

By Marta Bańbura, Domenico Giannone, Filippo Durero, Yiqiao Sun and Srečko Zimic

Scenario analysis is an increasingly important tool for monetary policy. This blog shows how scenario synthesis links narrative scenarios with probabilistic risk assessments, helping policymakers evaluate scenario relevance, coverage and shifts in risk perceptions as new information arrives.

Why are scenarios needed?

When the ECB’s Governing Council makes monetary policy decisions, it does not focus solely on the most likely forecast for inflation and growth. It must also assess the risks and uncertainty surrounding that outlook.

To do so, the ECB uses a range of methods and tools, including statistical risk measures based on past forecast errors or model output, an internal survey of staff views on risks known as the quantitative risk assessment (QRA) and sensitivity and scenario analysis. No single approach captures every aspect of uncertainty – each offers a different perspective on the risks facing the economy.[1]

Scenario analysis complements the statistical risk measures in two important ways.

First, it provides narratives. Scenarios explore specific risk events such as an energy shock, a geopolitical escalation, a trade disruption or tighter financial conditions, illustrating the economic channels through which they could affect inflation and growth.

Second, scenarios help policymakers examine developments that have little or no precedent. This is particularly important during periods of structural change or heightened geopolitical stress because risk measures based mainly on past events and data may understate the risks involved and the range of possible outcomes.

A clear example of this is the energy crisis following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The statistical measure of risks (illustrated in Chart 1 by a dark blue line) around the baseline Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) inflation forecast of 5.1% year-on-year, which was built on historical model residuals, did not capture the actual inflation outcome of 8.4% year-on-year. By contrast, the adverse and severe scenarios, respectively concentrated around 5.9% and 7.1%, highlighted risks that historical residuals alone would have missed. The scenarios helped broaden the range of possibilities considered in the policymaking process and brought the risk assessment closer to what ultimately occurred.

Scenario synthesis: linking narratives and risk measures

A scenario describes what could happen if a particular risk materialises. Once several scenarios are available, two questions arise: first, how relevant is each scenario and, second, how well do they collectively capture the overall risks?

To answer these questions, we need to link these scenarios to some overall and forward-looking reference measures of risk. Such measures may come from the ECB/Eurosystem’s QRA, options-implied probability distributions from financial markets or probability assessments from professional forecasters.[2] These measures show how markets, forecasters or policymakers see the balance of risks. However, they do not come with an underlying narrative; nor do they explain the relative importance of individual risk events or why risk perceptions are shifting.

Scenario synthesis provides that missing link.[3] It combines the baseline outlook with a set of alternative scenarios in a way that best matches one of these overall reference risk measures. In practice, this means deriving a synthetic distribution by assigning a weight to each scenario which reflects the degree to which it spans the range of relevant risks (Chart 2). Importantly, these weights should not be interpreted as probabilities that the scenarios will materialise. Rather, they indicate how relevant each scenario is for representing the risks contained in the reference measure, given the other scenarios included.

Applications

The ECB mainly uses the framework in three complementary ways.

First, it helps us assess the relative relevance of scenarios against different measures of risk.

Second, it allows us to track how scenario relevance evolves over time. For risk measures that update frequently, such as market-based measures, we can see whether a given scenario becomes more or less relevant as events unfold.

Third, it provides a way to assess whether the scenarios considered cover a sufficiently broad range of risks and to identify possible gaps.

Below, we illustrate each of the applications listed above. In all three examples, the distributions around the scenario paths are constructed using the ECB-BASE model.[4]

Linking Middle East conflict scenarios with different reference risk measures

To illustrate the first application, we examine the milder, adverse and severe alternative scenarios for the economic impact of the Middle East conflict in the June 2026 Eurosystem staff projections. We focus on euro area inflation and evaluate the scenarios against the risk assessments from financial markets and the Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF).[5]

Panel a of Chart 3 compares the reference risk measures with the baseline uncertainty based on historical model residuals. The market-based measure is more dispersed, with substantially more probability mass at higher inflation rates. By contrast, the SPF’s measure is close to the baseline, although somewhat narrower and with its peak at a slightly lower inflation rate. This highlights the different risk assessments reflected in market pricing and in professional forecasters’ views.

Panel b shows how the scenarios feature in each assessment: professional forecasters remain closest to the baseline. Meanwhile, financial markets place more weight on the adverse and severe scenarios.

Tracking evolving market-based risk perceptions

Scenario synthesis helps us understand how risk perceptions change as new information becomes available. We examine market-based risk measures for euro area inflation on 11 June, 30 June and 23 July (Chart 4).

Panel a shows that, by 30 June, around the announcement of a ceasefire in the Middle East, markets saw lower inflation risks than on 11 June, although uncertainty remained. By 23 July, however, renewed regional tensions heightened energy supply concerns. Consequently, market-based inflation risks rose again.

Panel b shows how the scenarios’ relative relevance changed: on 11 June the baseline mattered most, while the milder scenario became more relevant by 30 June as the balance of risks shifted in a more favourable direction. By 23 July, the geopolitical conflict had intensified further such that the adverse scenario was seen by the markets as most relevant. Overall, the chart shows how the scenario set spanned varying risk perceptions, with the relative relevance of individual scenarios adjusting to reflect new information.
