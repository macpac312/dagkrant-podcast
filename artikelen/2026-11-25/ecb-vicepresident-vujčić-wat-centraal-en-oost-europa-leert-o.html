Resilience in an age of shocks: lessons from central and eastern Europe

Speech by Boris Vujčić, Vice-President of the ECB, at the Conference on European Economic Integration (CEEI) 2026

This conference has long been associated with one of Europe’s most remarkable economic achievements: the convergence of the countries of central and eastern Europe (CEE).[1]

Since the mid-1990s, incomes in the region have more than doubled and the gap with advanced European economies has narrowed substantially (slide 2). EU accession and deeper integration into the Single Market were powerful forces behind this convergence, with openness being central to that success. Integration into European and global value chains supported productivity, while foreign direct investment accelerated capital accumulation and technology transfer.[2]

Yet the same openness that enabled convergence has also exposed CEE countries to repeated shocks. In the space of just a few years, Europe has faced a pandemic, Russia’s unjustified war against Ukraine, an unprecedented energy shock, disruptions to global supply chains and, more recently, renewed trade tensions and conflict in the Middle East.

These developments have placed economic resilience at the centre of the policy debate. And central and eastern Europe provides a particularly useful lens through which to think about what resilience means.

The experience of CEE economies provides valuable lessons for Europe as a whole. It shows us that openness and resilience are not opposites. In fact, integration can be a source of resilience through diversifying markets, suppliers and sources of financing. The question then is how to preserve the benefits of integration while strengthening our capacity to withstand and adapt to shocks.

So what actually makes an economy resilient? Resilience is not about avoiding shocks altogether – no economy can do that. Nor does it mean trying to become self-sufficient. Rather, resilience means having the buffers, institutions and flexibility to absorb shocks when they arrive, the capacity to recover afterwards and, ultimately, the ability to adapt to the new environment.

Today I would like to consider four dimensions of that resilience: monetary, fiscal, financial and structural.

Monetary resilience: credible policy provides the anchor

The inflation shock that started in 2021 was an extraordinary test for central banks, reminding us that inflation can return unexpectedly and forcefully (slide 3).

And it was particularly significant in central and eastern Europe, where food and energy tend to weigh more heavily in household consumption baskets. Many economies in the region are also relatively energy-intensive, magnifying their exposure to the initial shock. Tight labour markets and strong wage growth subsequently contributed to domestic price pressures.[3]

Divergent inflation developments can require different monetary policy responses. For a small open economy outside the monetary union, this can also lead to exchange rate movements and shifts in capital flows and financing conditions, potentially amplifying the original shock.

Credible central banks can help contain these risks.

The experience of recent years reminds us why price stability is itself a source of resilience (slide 4). When inflation expectations remain anchored and people trust the central bank to fulfil its mandate, an external supply shock is less likely to turn into persistent inflation.

But credibility cannot be taken for granted. It has to be earned. And that requires central banks to act when circumstances demand it, even when the decisions involved are difficult.

There is also a broader lesson to be learnt from the region’s experience: monetary resilience depends on the monetary and financial structures in which economies operate.

Here, participation in the euro area can provide important benefits.

For a highly integrated economy, joining the euro area removes exchange rate risk from its interactions with its main trading and financial partners. It eliminates the possibility of currency movements amplifying a shock through balance sheets or financing conditions. And it gives the economy access to the institutions and common monetary framework of the euro area.

Before euro adoption, the Croatian economy, like many in the region, was already highly “euroised”, with a large share of private and public liabilities denominated in euro. Joining the euro therefore removed an important source of foreign currency risk and strengthened the economy’s resilience to financial shocks.[4]

This does not mean that participation in the euro area fully insulates countries from shocks. We know very well that it does not. But it does remove one potential source of instability: the risk that exchange rate movements amplify those shocks. In periods of heightened uncertainty, these benefits become particularly valuable.

And this illustrates my first lesson: monetary resilience ultimately rests on credible institutions and a stable nominal anchor.

Fiscal resilience: building buffers before they are needed

The second dimension is fiscal resilience.

The principle here is simple: buffers need to be built in good times, before they are needed during downturns.

Fiscal policy played a crucial stabilising role during the pandemic and again during the energy crisis, helping to cushion the impact of exceptionally large shocks on households and firms. Importantly, countries with stronger public finances had greater room to respond.

Many CEE economies still benefit from comparatively low levels of public debt. This is an important source of resilience, but it requires continued efforts if it is to be preserved (slide 5).

When we look at fiscal flows, budget deficits have increased across much of the region in recent years, reflecting a combination of higher social and investment spending, rising public sector wage bills, higher interest rate expenditure and, increasingly, greater defence spending.

At the same time, headline fiscal positions do not tell the whole story. The structure of public debt and the strength of fiscal institutions also matter. In some non-euro area CEE economies, foreign currency exposures and reliance on non-resident investors remain significant, while contingent liabilities and weaknesses in fiscal governance can create additional vulnerabilities.[5]

Credible and sound fiscal policies, supported by strong fiscal frameworks, are therefore essential to preserve confidence and fiscal space, and also to reduce the risk that changes in market sentiment translate into abrupt increases in sovereign financing costs.

And while euro area membership can reduce risks related to euro-denominated public debt, it is no substitute for sound fiscal policies. Maintaining adequate fiscal buffers remains essential, including within a monetary union.[6]

So the lesson is straightforward: relatively low debt is a valuable asset for many CEE economies, but it is an asset that needs to be preserved. Fiscal resilience requires both sufficient buffers and credible institutions that help safeguard them.
