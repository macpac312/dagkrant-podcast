Strong, but not immune: resilience in an age of uncertainty

Maybe we can start with the aggregated results of the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP), which were presented this morning. Can you provide a brief overview of the key takeaways from the exercise this year?

The main takeaway is that the euro area banking sector remains broadly resilient, but this is no reason for complacency. The average SREP score remained stable at 2.5, and scores were unchanged for most banks. Around 8% of banks improved and 7% deteriorated. Over five years, however, scores have changed for more than half of the banks, showing that our assessments respond to changes in their risk profiles.

Capital requirements also remain broadly stable. Overall Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) requirements and guidance for 2027 stand at 11.3% of risk-weighted assets, compared with 11.2% for 2026. Over the past five years, they have risen from around 10.4%, almost entirely because national authorities raised macroprudential buffers. Our own microprudential requirements have barely moved: Pillar 2 requirements in CET1 have stayed at around 1.1-1.2% throughout, and Pillar 2 guidance is now lower than five years ago.

All banks currently hold capital above their applicable 2027 requirements, buffers and guidance, while the sector’s aggregate CET1 ratio stood at around 16% in the second quarter of 2026. How much banks hold above our requirements is for them to decide. We have clarified in our Guide to the internal capital adequacy assessment process (ICAAP) that the management buffer reflects a bank’s own view of the capital it needs to keep its business model sustainable. It is not a supervisory requirement. Banks also remain liquid and profitable, and asset quality has shown little deterioration so far despite the difficult geopolitical and macroeconomic environment.

This year, we applied our revised methodology for Pillar 2 requirements for the first time. It links the requirements more closely to banks’ underlying risks and accounts for potential overlaps with Pillar 1 following the final Basel III reforms. Where we identified overlaps, we adjusted Pillar 2 requirements to avoid double-counting.

At the same time, the aggregate picture should not hide persistent weaknesses. Internal governance and operational resilience remain key concerns, particularly risk data aggregation, ICT and cyber resilience, business continuity and third-party risk management. Credit risk management also continues to account for a large share of our qualitative measures. And although overall asset quality remains sound, pockets of vulnerability remain in consumer credit, commercial real estate and lending to energy-intensive or supply-chain-exposed firms.

So the overall message is one of resilience, but not immunity. Banks are navigating this period of heightened uncertainty from a strong position. They need to use that strength to address long-standing weaknesses and remain prepared for risks that may take time to materialise.

In the context of the SREP, how well prepared are supervisors and banks to address both financial risks, such as liquidity risk, and non-financial risks, such as cyber risk? Do you have sufficiently granular data to monitor and measure these risks at bank level? And how do you determine the appropriate supervisory response, including whether capital, liquidity or qualitative measures are best suited to address a particular risk?

We have a strong supervisory framework for assessing both financial and non-financial risks. Through the SREP, we combine regulatory reporting, targeted reviews, on-site inspections and stress tests to assess each bank’s exposures and risk management capabilities. Preparedness nevertheless varies across banks, and supervisory judgement remains essential as risks evolve.

For liquidity risk, we have detailed, standardised information on banks’ funding and liquidity positions. Banks’ aggregate liquidity coverage ratio stood at around 155% in the second quarter of 2026, comfortably above the regulatory minimum. At bank level, we examine funding concentrations, maturity profiles, available collateral and resilience to severe outflows, including in individual currencies. The 2026 geopolitical reverse stress test showed, for example, that some banks need to better capture the interaction between solvency and liquidity pressures.

For cyber risk, incident and loss data are useful, but cannot tell us how a bank would withstand the next attack. We therefore also assess security controls, governance, third-party dependencies and the ability to maintain or restore critical services. The Digital Operational Resilience Act has strengthened our information base through harmonised reporting of major ICT incidents and banks’ arrangements with ICT providers. Our 2024 cyber resilience stress test showed that banks had response and recovery frameworks in place, but it also identified shortcomings requiring further work.

Overall, we have extensive, granular information for bank-level supervision. At the same time, we want to avoid duplicate reporting. Over the past two years, we have reduced the number of data points banks provide for the SREP short-term exercise data by 20% and we work with regulators and other supervisors to streamline reporting requirements, without compromising our ability to identify and monitor risks.

Let me also emphasise that it is not only about how much information is availble or reported, but also about whether it is accurate, complete and available quickly enough primarily for the banks’ own executive and governance bodies to support decisions, especially under stress. Some banks still have weaknesses in aggregating and reporting risk data, affecting the management of both financial and non-financial risks. Addressing these weaknesses remains a supervisory priority.

Our response depends on the nature and severity of the risk, the safeguards already in place and the bank’s progress in addressing deficiencies. Additional capital covers risks not adequately captured by minimum requirements. Liquidity measures may require a bank to hold additional liquid assets so that it can meet payment obligations even during stressed outflows. Qualitative measures remedy weaknesses in controls, governance or risk management. These measures may be complementary: a bank may need capital to cover potential operational losses while also being required to strengthen its cyber controls and recovery capabilities. Capital provides protection against losses, but it does not remove the obligation to fix the underlying weakness.

A draft report on good practices in governance and risk management will be published in early 2027. It will offer banks illustrative examples of approaches that have proven effective in certain institutional contexts, helping them review their own arrangements and identify improvements suited to their circumstances. It is not intended to serve as a basis for supervisory findings or measures.

On the AI capex supercycle, what is your perspective as a supervisor on the financing and banks’ other exposures to it?We also get questions around the circularity of some of these exposures between the different players. How are you monitoring these linkages, as banks’ disclosures in this area are relatively poor?

The AI buildout may deliver substantial productivity gains, but it also comes with large financing needs. From a supervisory perspective, the central question is whether the financing remains sound if revenues develop more slowly than expected. Much of the initial investment has been undertaken by highly profitable technology companies with strong balance sheets. But as the investment cycle draws increasingly on debt and complex financing structures, exposure to potentially disappointing returns is widening.

Banks are connected to this cycle in several ways. They have direct exposures to technology companies involved in the AI supply chain and data centre development, but can also be indirectly exposed via non-bank financial intermediaries, which may be financing data centres through investment vehicles and private credit funds. A bank may therefore finance both a project and a fund investing in it. Banks need to capture these connections across products and business lines, including commitments to provide further financing. Banks’ current exposures are relatively contained but the indirect channel is difficult to estimate given the opacity of many of the funds. Euro area non-banks are also highly exposed, mainly through equity – and given growing interlinkages between banks and non-banks, these indirect exposures need to be considered too.

Circular financing deserves particular attention. A technology company may, for example, invest in an AI developer that uses the funding to purchase computing services from that same company. Such arrangements can have a sound commercial rationale, such as securing access to scarce computing capacity. But they also link valuations, financing and revenues. If end-user demand disappoints, several parts of the chain may come under pressure simultaneously. Banks therefore need to distinguish revenues based on sustainable customer demand from those that depend on continued financing within the network.

Sound underwriting is therefore essential. The risks differ depending on whether a bank is financing land acquisition, construction or an operational data centre. Banks need to assess the full financing cycle, test whether projected revenues and safeguards remain credible under adverse conditions, and reflect the results in their pricing and risk appetite. Given the specific risk characteristics of data centre financing, in 2027 we will carry out targeted on-site inspections on a small number of banks to verify whether origination standards are sound. This will complement the ongoing work by Joint Supervisory Teams.

Full visibility of the risks is difficult to achieve, particularly where financing is indirect, passes through complex private structures or across borders. Better data and information-sharing between authorities is therefore important. Banks themselves need a consolidated view of exposures and commitments across counterparties, products and business lines. They must identify where separate transactions depend on the same firms and cash flows, and assess the consequences if the assumptions underpinning the AI investment cycle prove too optimistic. These indirect contagion channels are also important for us to understand and are something that we will be focusing on in the coming months.

We are seeing diverging paths on regulation across regions, with some relaxation on the leverage ratio in the United States and more expected in other areas. For example, the top six US banks have grown their leverage exposure by USD 2.5-3 trillion in one year alone. How do you see this from the perspective of a European banking supervisor – do you see a competitive threat for European banks in capital-intensive businesses and, if so, how do you plan to address this?

Regulatory differences can affect competition, particularly where European and US banks compete directly. Where leverage requirements constrain activity, lowering them can release capacity for businesses such as repo financing and Treasury market intermediation, influencing pricing and market share. We take these effects seriously, including the impact of competitive pressures on banks’ business models and risk taking.

The exposure figures nevertheless need careful interpretation. Leverage exposure includes assets and off-balance-sheet positions without risk weighting. Its growth can reflect larger securities financing or derivatives positions, which may have very different risk implications. Assessing any competitive disadvantage also requires comparing the full frameworks, including risk-weighted and leverage requirements, buffers and stress testing, and how they apply at group and subsidiary level. Some changes in the United States reduce requirements that previously exceeded international standards. Their practical effect depends on which requirement is binding for a particular bank and activity.

Our response starts with international cooperation. Consistent implementation of agreed Basel standards supports fair competition and financial stability. Where differences materially affect comparable activities, we should examine the evidence in the Basel Committee and other international fora. The leverage ratio and output floor remain important safeguards against the underestimation of risk, and regulatory changes should be assessed by their combined effect on banks’ resilience.

But regulation is only one factor shaping competitiveness. Market scale, business mix and the depth of capital markets also matter. Europe should address these structural constraints and remove unnecessary complexity, rather than respond to regulatory divergence by weakening prudential standards.

Within our supervisory remit, we are making supervision more focused, efficient and predictable through a more targeted SREP, faster capital-related decisions and simpler reporting. We also support simplifying the European capital framework while preserving its overall resilience. The objective is to remove costs and complexity that serve no prudential purpose, while keeping supervisory requirements firmly grounded in banks’ risks.

Talking about global competitiveness, if we take the market cap of the top five US banks at this conference, it is bigger than the market cap of the entire European bank index. Does this matter to you as a supervisor? European banks are sub-scale in market-making and investment banking compared with their US counterparts, does this matter to the supervisor?
