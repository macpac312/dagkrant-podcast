Impact of demographic change on Europe’s economy, banking sector and supervision

Thank you for inviting me here today to discuss demographic change and its implications for Europe’s economy and banking sector. A year ago I wrote about Europe’s demographic shift and what it could mean for banking supervision. Since then, the case for examining this issue has not diminished. If anything, it has grown stronger. I would therefore like to circle back to this subject today, drawing on the latest data and considering more closely the story they tell about Europe’s economy, banking sector and banking supervision.

The demographic picture

Before turning to the data, let me be clear about what we are discussing. My aim is to simply look at demographic trends and their implications, not to pass judgement on them.

The decision to have children lies at the intersection of a person’s individual choice, health and economic circumstances, housing situation and broader social factors. For the purposes of this speech, the fertility rate is defined as the average number of children born per woman, which is a commonly used indicator of the renewal of generations. In the same vein, average life expectancy can vary depending on socio-economic status, gender and the quality of health and social security systems. Today I want to focus on the main outcome of these trends, which is the rising share of older people in the population.

In a blog post a year ago I noted that the fertility rate in many EU countries was around 1.5 births per woman.[1] The EU-wide figure has actually been below this level for some time and has continued to decline, falling from 1.46 in 2022 to 1.38 in 2023, and, according to data published by Eurostat this March, to 1.34 in 2024. This is the lowest level recorded since comparable EU statistics began in 2001. Fertility rates are even lower in several Member States. In Spain, for example, the rate is close to 1.1.

People are also having children later in life. A woman’s average age when she has her first child is now 29.9 years, while the average age at childbirth rose from 29.0 in 2001 to 31.3 in 2024.[2]

At the start of 2025, 22% of the EU population was aged 65 or over, compared with less than 18% 15 years earlier. There are now just over three people of working age for every person aged 65 or over.[3] Eurostat projects that by 2050 around three in ten people in the EU will be aged 65 or over.[4] The working-age population is projected to shrink by some 65 million people by the end of the century, while the number of people aged 80 and over is expected to increase by a factor of two and a half over the same horizon.[5]

Recent data suggest that demographic change may be advancing faster than many projections indicated only a year ago. The German Federal Statistical Office recently published a new population forecast that is significantly lower than its previous projection. Revised assumptions about net immigration to Germany are the main reason behind this change. In the past, net migration offset that gap. In 2025, however, it may no longer have done so[6].

At almost the same time, France’s National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies published its figures for 2025, revealing that for the first time since 1945, France’s natural population balance turned negative.

This demographic shift is occurring globally, albeit at very different stages. The world’s total fertility rate stood at 2.2 in 2024, down from 2.25 the year before. Projections have also changed substantially. Ten years ago the United Nations expected the global fertility rate to fall below the replacement threshold of 2.1 around 2073. Its latest assessment brings that date forward to 2050. More than half the world’s countries, home to over two-thirds of the global population, are already below the replacement fertility level, and many of these countries have already seen their populations peak.[7]

Parts of Asia illustrate how quickly populations can age. In Japan, the share of the population aged 65 or over is already close to 30% and is projected to reach around 38% by the middle of the century. In South Korea, it currently stands at roughly 20% and is set to approach 40%. Japan, Hong Kong, South Korea and mainland China are all already experiencing population decline, and Asia’s population as a whole is projected to peak around 2054.[8]

Demographic change is therefore a structural shift, although its timing and intensity vary across countries and have different implications for their economies.

Economic outcomes are not shaped by demographic projections alone. Migration, labour force participation, productivity and policy choices can all influence how demographic change affects the economy. But a population’s current age structure changes only gradually, meaning that demographic trends have a lasting influence, making them particularly relevant for long-term analysis.

Today I would like to explain why demographic trends are slow to reverse, before going on to consider how a smaller and older population could affect the wider economy. I would then like to look at the impact this could have on banks and their customers, before discussing how these developments could be incorporated into supervisory analysis.

Why demographic trends are slow to reverse

I would like to begin by highlighting that, owing to population momentum, even a significant increase in the fertility rate would take time to affect the size of the working-age population.

In ageing societies, the number of women of childbearing age shrinks. As a result, even a substantial increase in the fertility rate would initially only result in a limited increase in the number of births. Demographers refer to this as population momentum, and it can be illustrated with a few figures.

Most EU countries, as well as Japan, South Korea and China have a long history of low fertility. Across this group, the number of women of reproductive age fell from 669 million in 1994 to 634 million in 2024. This number is projected to decrease even further to around 474 million by 2054, a decline of 25% in just three decades. Births are therefore likely to continue falling even if fertility rates increase moderately. Based on the UN’s central projections, any increase in fertility rates would be partly offset by the continuing decline in the number of women of reproductive age.[9]

Public policies can support parents and help them reconcile family and working life. However, decisions about whether and when to have children depend on a broad range of personal, social and economic factors. The available evidence does not suggest that any single policy instrument can rapidly or substantially alter demographic trends.

Lower birth rates are only one part of the demographic shift. Longer life expectancy is also changing the composition of Europe’s population, leading to longer periods spent in retirement and making it increasingly important to support older people in remaining economically active for longer.

Europe’s workforce[10] and customer base over the next two to three decades will therefore be shaped largely by the population’s current age structure. Future decisions to have children will matter, but their economic effects will take considerable time to emerge. Demographic change is structural, and its effects will persist over the horizons relevant for banking supervision. Supervisors should therefore assess these developments before they become material vulnerabilities.

What a changing population means for the wider economy

Demographic change affects the wider economy in which banks operate.[11] The implications of this change for banks cannot be separated from broader shifts in household demand, labour supply and economic activity.

It is useful to distinguish between the effect of two demographic changes, i.e. that of a smaller population and that of an older population. The two can differ considerably, even within the same sector.

The effects will not be felt evenly across the economy. Housing markets may have fewer first-time buyers and, over time, a greater supply of homes in regions with declining populations. At the same time, longer life expectancy, structural housing shortages and affordability constraints are already delaying access to home ownership for many younger households. These demographic shifts may interact with housing markets in very different ways across regions.

Demand for health and long-term care is likely to rise, even as these sectors face acute labour shortages. Consumer spending may shift away from goods associated with younger families towards health, mobility and leisure. By contrast, education systems will need to adjust to declining student numbers, with potential consequences for the future workforce and research capacity.

Population ageing may also lead to difficult political choices regarding the composition of public expenditure. Without more people working, increased productivity or policy changes, an increase in spending related to ageing populations could reduce the resources available for education, research and infrastructure, potentially weighing on future economic growth.

This could have particularly significant consequences for human capital and innovation. A recent European Commission foresight exercise projects around 2.5 million fewer higher education students in the EU by 2050 and a labour force that is around 12% smaller.[12]

Analysis by the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) outlines the main channels through which ageing may affect growth. In the EU, the Western Balkans and the eastern European economies covered by the study, population ageing alone is projected to reduce annual growth in GDP per capita by an average of 0.36 percentage points between now and 2050.

One way in which ageing may affect growth is through innovation. As the EBRD notes, a shrinking pool of researchers is already acting as a headwind to innovation in ageing economies. These estimates do not account for any future productivity gains from the effective use of artificial intelligence.[13]

My ECB colleague Isabel Schnabel recently made a related point from a monetary policy perspective. She argued that demographic ageing, moderating immigration and other structural factors are making labour increasingly scarce and contributing to wage pressures in the euro area. At the same time, she noted that productivity gains from artificial intelligence could, over time, help ease these supply-side constraints, although both their timing and magnitude remain highly uncertain.[14]
