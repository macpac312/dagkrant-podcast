Acting in time

Keynote speech by Christine Lagarde, President of the ECB, at the 36th Frankfurt European Banking Congress

It is a pleasure to be back at this congress. This is the eighth time I have spoken here as President of the ECB, and it will sadly be the last.

In preparing for today, I reread those speeches. The topics have varied, from growth and inflation to capital markets and the Single Market.

But one concern runs through all of them: how to make Europe stronger, so that its prosperity is driven by its own strengths and less exposed to decisions taken elsewhere.

For many years, the challenges we discussed here were old ones. We had fallen short of our ambitions for the Single Market. Our growth relied too heavily on external demand for an economy of our size, and our competitiveness was slowly eroding.

Those challenges have not gone away. But in recent years, new ones have been layered on top. Dependencies that we once saw as a normal part of an open economy, from critical raw materials to technology, can now be used against us. That has made economic sovereignty a challenge in its own right.

The old challenges and the new ones have something in common.

Five centuries ago, Machiavelli compared the problems of states to a fever that, at the start, is “easy to cure but difficult to detect” and, in time, becomes “easy to detect but difficult to cure”.

Both kinds of challenge are of this sort. By the time everyone can see the need to act, the best time to act has usually passed.

Acting early, before the case is obvious, is one of the hardest things a policymaker has to do. It means paying a price up front for a distant pay-off. And if nothing goes wrong, those who acted can easily appear to have paid that price for nothing.

Today I would like to reflect on how Europe can still act early, drawing on how we have approached this at the ECB and on what I have learned along the way.

Why Europe acts in some crises but not others

It is often said that Europe only moves in a crisis. Jean Monnet wrote that Europe would be forged in crises, and would be the sum of the solutions adopted for them. The idea was that Europe has a kind of in-built inertia that only a crisis can overcome.

My experience of the past seven years is that this is only partly true. Europe responds decisively to some crises and hesitantly to others.

When events convince us that the world has changed, we act. During my term, I have watched Europe break one taboo after another.

Before the pandemic, few believed that the EU would ever borrow on a large scale in its own name. But once we accepted that no country could recover on its own, we created a recovery fund financed by common debt to the tune of €800 billion.

Europe was also said to be unable to take its own security seriously. Yet when Germany concluded that it would have to take on more of its own defence, it made the biggest change to its defence spending in generations. At the European level, we also agreed on Security Action for Europe (SAFE), which offers up to €150 billion in loans for joint defence procurement. And after decades of neutrality, Finland and Sweden joined NATO.

Nor did many expect Europe to act as a geopolitical power. Yet it is now Ukraine’s largest supporter. And when it became clear that Europe would have to bear more of the cost, the countries ready to act found a new way to borrow together on Ukraine’s behalf, even though not every country was ready to take part. Hence the coalition of the willing.

What made these steps possible? In each case, the cost of inaction was there for all to see, and it was plainly greater than the cost of acting. Once we were convinced of that, our institutions did not hold us back.

Our difficulties arise when a crisis moves slowly. The cost of inaction is then just as real, but it is far less visible. It builds up a little each year, and no single moment forces a decision. Meanwhile, the cost of acting is certain and has to be paid straight away.

Both the old challenges and the new ones are of this kind.

Take capital markets. We have all agreed for more than a decade that Europe needs a capital markets union. But building it means taking on vested interests now, for gains that arrive years later.

Each year without it, more of our savings are invested abroad and more of our growing firms look elsewhere for funding. Yet no single year’s loss is large enough to overcome the resistance to change.

We have seen this again in recent weeks. The Council has just agreed its position on strengthening the supervision of capital markets, which is welcome. But the position weakens the European supervisor’s ability to take effective decisions and includes too many carve-outs.

Our dependencies raise the same problem. Reducing them means paying for new capacity now, against a threat that may never materialise. One recent estimate suggests that raising Europe’s share of the world’s AI computing capacity from around 5% to 15% by 2030 would require investment of around €1.3 trillion.[1]

But as long as we can buy computing power from foreign providers and frontier AI models remain available to us, the case for paying that price is hard to make.

In both, the cost of inaction is real, whether it is a loss we are already suffering or a danger we may face tomorrow. But it rarely becomes visible enough to build the broad consensus for change that we found for the pandemic or for Ukraine.

This is the central problem of European policymaking. We can see the problem early, but we can act only once it has become impossible to ignore. And by then, almost by definition, it is too late. Lost industries are very hard to win back, and once a dependency has been used against us, our sovereignty is already exposed.

Think of the euro area debt crisis. The scale of Greece’s difficulties was clear long before Europe acted decisively to strengthen its institutions. We paid a much higher price than if we had acted earlier, including a six-quarter recession.

We often hear that policymakers in Europe do not understand the problems they face. I think they do. The difficulty is that they are asked to impose visible costs on their citizens today, for benefits that will only become apparent over time, before a majority is convinced that action is needed.
