If time stood still: regulatory modernisation and financial stability

Introduction

Suppose we suddenly decided to leave all banking rules and supervisory practices unchanged for the next ten years.[1] Could we justify that decision?

The financial system would continue to evolve. Banks would adopt new technologies and business models. Some safeguards might remain effective. Others might leave us exposed to risks they were never designed to address.

Meanwhile, banks and supervisors would need to continue spending time and money on requirements that had lost their usefulness, or on work that technology could do faster and cheaper.

Doing nothing would therefore leave us with unnecessary costs and less protection. But that conclusion alone does not tell us what we need to change.

To make the right choices, we need to start with the purpose of our work: ensuring the safety and soundness of banks and the stability of the financial system. People depend on banks every day. Households entrust banks with their savings; they rely on them to make payments and to finance their homes. Businesses need banks to manage cashflows such as wages and to finance investment. All in all, banks provide services that are fundamental to the economy, and any disruption can lead to lost jobs, failed businesses and lasting economic damage.

And this is where regulatory and supervisory modernisation must also start. It must help preserve banks’ ability to provide these services, including when conditions deteriorate. We welcome the Financial Stability Board’s (FSB) recently published draft principles[2] and support their overall direction. They are closely aligned with the reforms we are already pursuing at the ECB: simplifying processes, adopting new technologies and tools responsibly, sharpening our focus on the most important risks and preserving resilience.

The FSB consultation gives us all an opportunity to share our experiences, learn from one another and examine together where our approaches can be improved. I encourage all stakeholders, including financial institutions, researchers and civil society organisations, to contribute their evidence and experience to this public consultation.

The question I want to explore today is therefore: how can we keep a changing financial system safe while reducing unnecessary complexity and costs?

I will address this question in three parts.

First, what does it mean to keep the financial system safe?

Second, how can we keep our safeguards effective as risks evolve?

Third, how do we know if a change is actually an improvement?

What does it mean to keep the financial system safe?

Keeping the financial system safe does not mean eliminating risk. Banking involves taking risks. A business can make a sound investment and still fail. A bank can lend responsibly and still incur losses. An economy in which no one can take such risks will struggle to grow.[3]

But when investments fail and losses arise, the wider economy still needs a functioning banking system. To quote the FSB’s draft report: “financial stability provides the foundation for sustainable economic growth, which in turn supports financial stability”.

Safety therefore requires banks to manage the risks they take and have the resources to withstand losses and episodes of funding stress, all while maintaining essential operations.

This is where capital and liquidity requirements come into play. Capital absorbs losses, helping banks remain solvent and continue lending under stress. It helps protect depositors and reduces the risk that taxpayers will have to support a failing bank. Similarly, liquidity requirements help ensure that banks can meet payments as they fall due, including under stress. Together, these safeguards support confidence in the financial system.

In addition, banks must understand and manage the risks they take. Banks’ boards and management are responsible for putting in place appropriate internal governance and risk management arrangements. Strong governance is central to banks’ ability to recognise and respond to changing risks – a point I will return to later.

Even with these safeguards, banks can still fail. We therefore also need arrangements to manage failures in an orderly way, protecting depositors and essential services.[4]

Supervision contributes to economic growth by helping banks remain safe and sound. Resilient banks are better placed to finance productive investment through good times and bad, including when losses rise or funding becomes harder to obtain.

But we should be clear about what that contribution entails. Finance can help fund the investment needed to boost productivity; it cannot replace the structural reforms needed to make that investment profitable and productive. Europe needs a more integrated Single Market, conditions that support innovation and the adoption of new technologies, and fewer barriers to firms expanding across borders.[5] The evidence shows that capital is not currently a major constraint on euro area lending. In the ECB’s latest bank lending survey,[6] banks attributed tighter corporate credit standards mainly to heightened risk perceptions and lower risk tolerance.[7] Supervisors cannot resolve Europe’s structural growth challenges simply by adjusting prudential requirements.

How can we keep our safeguards effective as risks evolve?

Sustaining banks’ ability to finance the economy means keeping our safeguards effective as the risks they face change. That requires us to look at banks within the wider financial system.

Geopolitical tensions and trade barriers can result in lower economic growth and higher inflation, weakening borrowers and disrupting markets. Their effects may take time to show up on banks’ balance sheets. High public debt, weak growth and rising spending needs can limit governments’ capacity to cushion such shocks. Climate change continues to challenge the banks and the wider economy, as illustrated by the increasing frequency of extreme weather events. Rapid technological developments and rising cyber threats are also reshaping the risks banks need to manage.

I want to highlight three features of this risk environment that matter most for modernisation: the way risks interact, the way exposures cross sectoral boundaries, and the way some risks can materialise very rapidly.

First, the way risks interact. A sharp rise in government bond yields could trigger losses across portfolios and increase borrowers’ financing costs. Concentrated positions, excess leverage and liquidity mismatches can amplify such market shocks by forcing investors to sell into falling markets.[8]

Our recent reverse stress test on geopolitical risk shows why banks need to capture risk interactions in their assessments. We asked banks to identify plausible geopolitical scenarios that would lead to a substantial decline in their capital ratios. Some banks projected only limited liquidity stress. Yet losses can also undermine confidence and make funding harder to obtain. Assessing risks separately can therefore underestimate their combined impact.[9]

Second, the way exposures cross sectoral boundaries. Consider the growing use of debt to finance investment in artificial intelligence (AI), which is broadening the range of lenders and investors exposed if expected returns disappoint. Private credit is one such source of financing. But limited transparency can make it difficult to establish where leverage sits and who would ultimately bear losses. Banks may be exposed through lending both to funds and to the companies financed by those funds.[10] More broadly, AI may reshape firms’ competitiveness and employment, affecting borrowers’ repayment capacity well beyond the technology sector. However, the timing and scale of these effects remain uncertain.[11]

Third, the way some risks can materialise very rapidly. A cyberattack on a technology or critical service provider serving multiple financial institutions could disrupt services across the system. Frontier AI models can also shorten the gap between the discovery of a software vulnerability and its exploitation, leaving less time to respond.[12]

Whatever the risk, protection starts with a well-run bank. Sound governance, risk management and risk culture shape how banks deal with every kind of risk. They help ensure that assumptions are challenged, warnings are heard and follow-up is undertaken. They matter more when conditions change: banks need to recognise when familiar approaches no longer provide adequate protection and must be ready and willing to adapt. Reflecting on the banking turmoil of 2023, Andrea Enria concluded: “Governance was at the root of the problem”.[13]
