Massive New Study Shows How Young People Feel About AI Companions

A new study from Gallup looked at how young people across the world are making friends, both digitally and in person. The good news is that around 93% of the young people surveyed reported having at least one strong in-person friendship. The weird news is that 33% said they had an AI companion.

Gallup’s study is lengthy and nuanced. It partnered with the Boston Children’s Digital Wellness Lab to conduct the study and used online surveys of more than 16,000 young people from the ages of 15 to 22 in 16 different countries.

A lot of the topline results won’t surprise anyone living in today’s constantly online digital-forward age. Gallup found that respondents felt their in-person friendships were their strongest, that digital friendships complemented rather than replaced in-person ones, and that stronger friendships made people happier no matter how the connection was made. Three things that stood out were how the young respondents talked about AI, loneliness, and doomscrolling.

Young people in the United States, Canada, and UK reported the least interest in AI overall, with only 20% saying they used it once a day or more. Almost 40 percent of young people in Brazil use it daily. Japan and Italy both come in around 35%. “Nearly half of young people in the United States, United Kingdom and Canada have used AI no more than a few times in the past month, including those who have not used it at all,” the researchers wrote.

Most of the respondents (82%) said they’d used AI to look up information, 68% asked it for advice, and 33% used it as a companion. Young people with online-only friends were more likely to use AI for companionship, “although these patterns do not establish whether AI companionship supplements or substitutes for human relationships,” they wrote.

The researchers discovered that people in different countries felt wildly different about their AI buddies. Almost half of Australians with AI friends rated the relationship as “high quality” and only 12% of young people in Poland did. Mostly, though, people thought of their AI friends as mid: “In most countries [...] young people are more likely to give their AI companionship a medium rating than either a high or low rating.”

People who used AI as a friend also reported higher levels of loneliness, the study found. “However, the findings cannot determine whether young people turn to AI because they feel lonely, whether AI companionship influences their social experiences or whether other circumstances help explain these relationships,” the researchers wrote.

The researchers also asked young people what they do online all day and the overwhelming majority (72%) said they spend their time scrolling short form content, mostly TikTok and Instagram. It’s also how they deal with loneliness, with six in 10 young people participating in this study reporting that they often or always scroll through online content when lonely.

They also said scrolling works to dispel loneliness almost equally as well as hanging out with a friend in real life: “Although scrolling is more commonly used when young people feel lonely, those who use scrolling or in-person time as coping strategies report nearly identical levels of perceived helpfulness.”

Still, people who reported strong in-person friendships said they felt lonely less often, while online friendships have the same effect on loneliness regardless of how participants ranked the quality of those friendships. “No comparable difference appears across levels of online-only friendship quality,” the authors wrote. Having in-person relationships seemed to be what mattered most: “Higher loneliness is also more common among young people who frequently communicate online with strangers or online-only friends than among those who frequently communicate with in-person friends or family members.”

The relationships between loneliness, online friendships, and AI is complex and a person’s material conditions shape how they form friendships more than their access to a chatbot or how they use social media. The researchers’ analysis of young people who said they have no trusted adults in their lives makes that clear.

“Further analysis identifies several characteristics associated with having none of these friendship modes, but one stands out in particular: not having a trusted adult outside the family,” the researchers wrote. “This association remains substantial after accounting for country or residence, gender, age, urbanicity, and parent/ caregiver relationship quality.”

The study’s authors warned about reading too much into these results. “These assessments should be compared cautiously with those for human friendships examined in this report, as young people may have different expectations of what makes an AI companion or human friendship meaningful or supportive,” they wrote. In general, people don’t have the same expectations of a chatbot that they do someone they know in real life.

“The broader message is not that one form of connection is inherently better or worse, but that the quality of young people’s relationships and the wider social context in which they exist matter greatly,” the study concluded.
