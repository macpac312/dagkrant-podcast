OpenAI asks outside experts, including a Nobel laureate, to check proofs its AI produced in math, computer science, and physics

OpenAI says its internal models have disproved a central assumption in computer science and proved part of one of the Millennium Prize Problems, among other results. Outside experts will now decide whether the proofs hold up.

OpenAI laid out three results from math, computer science, and physics in a blog post. The company says its internal AI models produced the proofs. OpenAI calls the manuscripts a kind of raw research data that still needs to be understood.

So OpenAI asked outside experts to act as "shepherds" for the results. Based on their advice, the company is handling each field differently. OpenAI says it also translated the math and computer science proofs into Lean.

Lean is a programming language that lets a computer check every step of a proof. That doesn't guarantee the result is error-free, though. Lean only checks whether the proof establishes the statement as it was written in Lean.

Experts still have to decide whether that statement actually captures the intended problem. If OpenAI is right, a core assumption in computer science is wrong The computer science result centers on the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH).

It concerns the Boolean satisfiability problem, or SAT. SAT deals with a logical formula whose variables can only be true or false. The question is whether you can set the variables so the whole formula comes out true.

With only a few variables, you can just try every combination. But each new variable doubles the number of possibilities. With 100 variables, you're already looking at a 31-digit number.

Put simply, the ETH says there's no real shortcut. You can speed up the trial and error, but in the worst case, computing time still grows exponentially, increasing by a fixed factor with every added variable.

OpenAI says the satisfiability problem is closely tied to the P versus NP problem. That's the famous open question of whether a problem whose solution can be checked quickly can also be solved quickly.

With SAT, checking is easy because you just plug in a proposed answer and do the math. The hard part is finding one that works. SAT is considered the textbook example of hard problems of this kind.

A fast method for solving it would instantly make thousands of other problems fast to solve too. Most experts believe no such method exists. That belief goes by the shorthand "P does not equal NP." The ETH goes further than that conjecture.

It says not only that SAT is hard, but how hard. If the ETH falls, P could still differ from NP, so hard problems wouldn't automatically become easy. The ETH was widely accepted as true.

Hundreds of papers in graph theory, computational biology, databases, and computational geometry build on it. They show, for example, that a problem can't be solved faster than a certain time if the ETH holds.

Now OpenAI says its system has proved the ETH is false. If the proof holds, those papers lose their foundation. Their results wouldn't automatically be wrong, but they'd no longer be proven.

OpenAI says the result could help researchers find better algorithms. Turning it into practical gains would still take a lot of research, though. The practical payoff is unclear, but the theoretical fallout would be big SAT matters a lot in practice.

Specialized programs called SAT solvers check chip designs for errors and test software, for example. They often handle formulas with millions of variables already. That's because formulas from real-world applications are usually much easier than the worst case the ETH deals with.

Speeding up only the worst case wouldn't automatically help these programs. MIT professor Ryan Williams struck a similarly cautious tone on X. When a user asked what everyday developers would notice first, Williams said nobody knows yet.

At the current pace, he said, it's plausible that practical SAT solving could see serious progress within 12 months. "Or we may see nothing of the sort. I really don't know," he admitted.

The result would also ripple through complexity theory itself, specifically the study of circuits, which are networks of simple logic gates like AND, OR, and NOT. If OpenAI's result holds, Williams says it would follow that a certain class of very demanding problems can't be solved with small circuits. "Small" here means that when the input doubles in length, the circuit needs at most about twice as many gates.

The people who came up with the ETH are checking whether it's wrong Williams will independently check and explain the result together with Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi of UC San Diego.

Impagliazzo and Paturi originally proposed the ETH along with Francis Zane. Williams writes that the group hasn't signed any nondisclosure agreements, is working unpaid, and plans to share interim findings as they go.

In an open letter, the three also make clear that their involvement isn't an endorsement of the claim. A result this big needs careful, independent review, they write. At the group's request, OpenAI is publishing all the raw data, including the written proof and the Lean proof.

The company has also committed to funding a conference on what the result means for the field. A decades-old wave conjecture may now hold in every dimension The math result concerns a conjecture about waves like those in sound, light, or earthquakes.

Mathematician Christopher Sogge proposed it in 1991. Waves can concentrate at a single spot, much like light at the focal point of a magnifying glass. The conjecture says this extreme concentration only lasts briefly.

Averaged over time, waves behave more smoothly, or evenly, than the extreme case would suggest. That's why it's called the local smoothing conjecture. Until now, it had only been proven in two dimensions, for waves on a surface like water.

Guth, Wang, and Zhang did that in 2019. OpenAI says its models have now produced a proof for every dimension, including waves in space and in hypothetical spaces with even more dimensions.

OpenAI says the result reaches beyond waves. Because the local smoothing conjecture is so basic to the field, two other well-known conjectures follow from it. They are the Kakeya conjecture from geometry and Stein's restriction conjecture from Fourier analysis.

Fourier analysis breaks signals like sounds into individual oscillations. Proving the local smoothing conjecture automatically proves both of these as well. The Kakeya conjecture shows how hard even these follow-on results are.
