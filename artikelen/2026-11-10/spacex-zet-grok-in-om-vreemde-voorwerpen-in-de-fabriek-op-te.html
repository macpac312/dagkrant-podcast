Grok at SpaceX: 5 Details That Matter on FOD Detection

SpaceX's VP of Launch has confirmed that xAI's Grok is now actively deployed on the factory floor — not for scheduling or emails, but for one of aerospace's most safety-critical tasks: finding foreign object debris inside rocket components. The accuracy gap between Grok and human inspectors is significant enough that it signals a real shift in how high-stakes quality control gets done.

### 1. What FOD Actually Is — and Why It Can Kill a Mission

Foreign Object Debris (FOD) refers to any material — metal shavings, wire fragments, fasteners, debris — left inside aerospace components during manufacturing or assembly. In a rocket engine or pressurized tube, a single overlooked fragment can cause catastrophic failure. The standard detection method involves a human inspector using a borescope (a flexible camera) to visually scan the interior of tubes and cavities. It's painstaking, time-consuming, and, as SpaceX's own data now shows, not perfect.

### 2. The Accuracy Gap Is Larger Than It Looks

Grok is scoring 99.9% accuracy on FOD detection versus 94% for human inspectors, according to SpaceX VP of Launch as reported by Sawyer Merritt. That 5.9-percentage-point gap sounds modest until you apply it to volume. If SpaceX runs thousands of borescope inspections per rocket build, a 6% miss rate compounds into a meaningful number of undetected debris events. At 99.9%, the miss rate drops to roughly one in a thousand — a fundamentally different risk profile for a program where margins are measured in milliseconds and millimeters.

### 3. The Workflow: Human + AI, Not AI Replacing Human

The VP of Launch described the process as augmented inspection, not full automation. A human inspector still physically performs the borescope — inserting the camera, navigating the tube, capturing footage. Grok then analyzes what the camera sees and flags potential debris. This keeps a trained human in the loop for the physical task while offloading the pattern-recognition burden to the model. It's a practical deployment model that sidesteps the regulatory and safety complications of fully autonomous inspection on flight hardware.

### 4. Grok Was Trained on SpaceX Engineering Data

This deployment isn't Grok being applied off-the-shelf. According to background research, Grok 4.7 — announced around September 2026 with 2.1 trillion parameters — was reportedly trained on SpaceX engineering data, including rocket development records. That domain-specific training is likely a key reason the model performs well on a task as specialized as aerospace FOD identification. A general-purpose vision model would face a steeper learning curve on borescope imagery; a model that has ingested SpaceX's own manufacturing documentation has relevant context baked in.

### 5. This Is Part of a Broader SpaceX AI Integration Push

The FOD detection deployment sits inside a larger strategic move. xAI was acquired by SpaceX and is being rebranded as SpaceX AI, with Grok expanding across enterprise workflows. SpaceX launched its Super Intelligence Partner Network in October 2026, with over 130 companies — including Nvidia, Snowflake, and Databricks — in early access. Grok Bot, the enterprise agent product, had over 800,000 paying weekly users by October 2026. The FOD use case is a concrete, high-visibility proof point that Grok can perform in safety-critical industrial environments — exactly the kind of reference case that accelerates enterprise adoption beyond SpaceX's own walls.

The borescope application is a narrow but telling example of where AI inspection tools are heading in aerospace and manufacturing. If Grok's accuracy holds at scale and across different component geometries, the pressure on other launch providers and aerospace manufacturers to adopt similar AI-assisted inspection will be hard to ignore. The question isn't whether AI will become standard in FOD detection — it's how quickly the rest of the industry catches up.
