Resilience of the euro area economy – weathering one shock, preparing for the next

By Óscar Arce, Gerrit Koester and Beatrice Pierluigi[1]

Despite a sharp rise in energy prices in early 2026, the euro area economy has proved resilient. This blog examines how decreased vulnerability to energy shocks, policy support and the global AI upswing have helped cushion the impact, and explores what is needed to sustain that resilience.

The latest shock follows years of economic turbulence. The euro area has navigated the pandemic and post-pandemic global supply shortages, the energy crisis following Russia’s war against Ukraine, Red Sea disruptions to global shipping, and trade tensions linked to US tariffs.[2] Although distinct, the events of early 2026 posed a stern, and still ongoing, challenge to the euro area's resilience. These events disrupted global energy markets amid geopolitical uncertainty, which can have significant and durable adverse effects on activity and amplify business cycle fluctuations.[3]

A series of shocks

Since 2022 euro area GDP has continued to follow a moderate upward trajectory, despite successive shocks.[4] Adverse shocks have not resulted in declines in output (Chart 1, panel a)[5], even though growth has slowed compared with the long-term average. So far, the latest energy shock has been no exception.

When excluding the volatile Irish data, real GDP in the second quarter of 2026 was 0.6% above the level reached in the fourth quarter of 2025. Incoming data point to a continued expansion. Compared with the previous quarter, the euro area economy (excluding Ireland) has fared better than forecasts expected, with a stable growth rate of around 0.3% in each of the last four quarters (Chart 1, panel b).

This raises an important question: why has the latest energy price shock had only a limited impact on the euro area economy so far?

The concept of economic resilience can help answering this question. It encompasses three complementary dimensions: absorptive capacity, the ability to limit the immediate impact of a shock; recovery capacity, or the ability to return quickly to the pre-shock path; and adaptive capacity, which refers to the ability to adjust economic structures and institutions in ways that reduce vulnerability to future shocks.

The experience of the latest energy shock illustrates how all three dimensions have strengthened in the euro area in recent years, although each also has its limits. It is important to distinguish between lasting structural improvements and support from policy measures and the AI-driven upswing, which may prove more temporary.

Is the economy less vulnerable to energy shocks?

The conflict in the Middle East initially sent oil prices soaring, but they eased after the June US-Iran memorandum of understanding (Chart 2, panel a). A second wave of the shock starting in July saw another significant increase in oil prices, along with sharp increases in refining margins and a sustained rise in gas prices.

The euro area economy has become structurally less exposed to fluctuations in fossil energy commodity prices. This explains why the impact of the latest energy shock has so far been relatively limited. Over the past three decades, firms and households have improved energy efficiency and reduced reliance on oil and gas[6].

Economic activity has gradually shifted towards less energy-intensive sectors, particularly services. The euro area economy is for example only half as reliant on oil as it was in the early 1990s (Chart 2, panel b).

ECB analysis suggests that this decline has reduced the impact of oil supply shocks on growth, consumption and investment in the euro area, making the economy more resilient.[7] The green transition can reinforce this development, as a growing share of renewable energy reduces the dependence on imported fossil fuels.[8]

This shift is increasingly shaping electricity prices in the euro area. The expansion of renewable generation capacity is reducing wholesale electricity prices in many countries.[9] This is linked to a higher share of renewables reducing the share of hours in which gas-fired plants set the marginal price of electricity, decreasing the pass-through from wholesale gas to electricity prices.

In Spain, for example, where renewable electricity generation is particularly high, the share of hours in which gas plants set electricity prices has fallen from around 80% in 2020 to just above 20% in 2026, considering the first nine months of each year. A similar decline has occurred in France, where nuclear power often dominates the generation mix, with the share falling from around 60% to around 20%.[10] Unlike during the 2021-22 energy shock, when electricity prices closely followed gas prices, the latest shock has had a smaller impact in countries with larger renewable and nuclear energy shares in the first half of 2026.[11]

Although Europe remains exposed to developments in global energy markets, the economy is now less vulnerable to fossil fuel and gas price shocks compared with a few decades ago.

At the same time, the public sector has been playing a larger role in the economy in the post-pandemic period, acting as a buffer against the impact of the energy shock linked to the conflict in the Middle East.

Chart 3, panel a, shows that non-market services have been consistently growing above their long-term average and faster than other sectors in recent years. And fiscal policy has again helped to absorb the energy shock.[12] Fiscal support of the economy, which can be measured by the fiscal stance, has been increasing over the year. While the December 2025 projections expected an expansionary fiscal stance of 0.25 percentage points for 2026, this has gradually increased to reach 0.5 percentage points, thus doubling the expected fiscal support for the euro area economy over 2026 (Chart 3, panel b).[13] This mainly reflects higher spending on defence and infrastructure in Germany, and to a lesser extent, fiscal support measures of euro area countries aimed to cushion the impact of higher energy costs.

However, part of this support is temporary, fiscal space is limited in several countries, and the projections point to less fiscal support in the coming years. Therefore, fiscal policy might not be able to cushion further shocks to the same extent.

Which factors supported growth?

Four factors that helped the economy get back on track stand out: a moderation in the degree of uncertainty perceived at the initial stages of the Middle East crisis, a more favourable external environment driven partly by AI, an AI-related boost to domestic investment, and a resilient labour market.
