Claude Duet vs Claude Cowork: One Pulls, One Pushes

There is a moment where the choice makes itself, and it is not a moment about features. You have a folder of regional exports and a budget file, and you want a spreadsheet back with the variances flagged and a note on what would not reconcile. That is a job you hand over, and Claude Cowork is the surface built for it: it works in the folders and applications you choose, shows each step, and hands back finished work for your review. Then there is the other morning. You did not ask for anything, and something still arrived — a brief, a flagged thread, a draft waiting on your approval. That is Claude Duet, running the same underlying Claude Opus 5.5 model inside its own virtual machine, checking your connected accounts on a timer, bringing you work instead of waiting for it. Same account, same plan, opposite direction of initiative. Everything else in this comparison hangs off that one axis, which is why it is worth naming before either product gets compared feature by feature.

The distinction that does all the work

Cowork is pull. You have a task, you find the folder, you state the goal rather than the method, and you come back later. Anthropic's own framing on the Cowork page is "Say what, not how," and the page is explicit that the interface assumes a handoff: "Switch to Claude Cowork when you want to hand off a task. Find it next to Chat." The product's centre of gravity is the task you already know you have.

Duet is push. Nobody hands it anything. It opens a research preview by waitlist today, starting with US Pro and Max subscribers, and the thing it does first is generate a brief every morning without being asked. It also checks your connected accounts every few hours, and Anthropic's announcement is unusually candid about why: checking nonstop would use up your plan's limits much faster. That sentence is a design decision wearing a limitation's clothes. Duet is not an always-on crawler; it is a scheduled observer that decides what deserves your attention and then tells you.

Once you accept that spine, the rest of the differences stop looking arbitrary. Push has to run somewhere that is not your laptop, so Duet has its own virtual machine and its own browser. Pull can afford to live inside your machine, so Cowork runs in the folders you point it at and can be installed or not. Push has to decide what matters without you, so Duet's approval gate sits in front of consequential actions. Pull has you in the loop by construction, so Cowork's gate sits in front of the one thing that cannot be undone — deletion.

What pull looks like when you are the one pulling

Cowork's shape is worth describing precisely, because "agent that does your work" hides how much of the control stays with you. It works directly in folders and tools you choose, which means no copying out of a chat and pasting into files. It shows each step as it happens — the files it opens, the tools it uses, the choices it makes — and you can redirect it mid-run from wherever you are. It runs on web, desktop, and mobile as the same product, and closing your laptop does not stop a task; Anthropic's phrasing is "Claude works when you don't." Big projects get split into chunks that run in parallel while it drafts.

The scheduling model is yours to set: any cadence, unattended, and you come back to the deck, the spreadsheet, or the research. That is the exact opposite of Duet's cadence, which arrives because Duet decided, not because you configured it.

The built-in browser is the detail most comparisons skip and the one that matters operationally. When a task needs a site opened, a page read, or a form filled, Cowork opens a browser in its own side panel rather than in yours, so your tabs, your sessions, and your logged-in accounts stay untouched while you keep working. It is separate from your own browser, logins, and tabs. You can sign in once per session, or import cookies to stay signed in, with Chrome, Edge, and Firefox supported on macOS and Firefox on Windows and Linux. Available in the Cowork desktop app on Pro, Max, and Team plans.

Pricing sits where the rest of Anthropic's consumer plans sit: Pro at 17 dollars per month on an annual subscription or 20 billed monthly, Max 5x at 100, Max 20x at 200, Team at 20 dollars per seat for teams of two to 150. Cowork is included rather than metered separately, which sets up the economics point later.

What push changes about your morning

Duet inverts almost every one of those decisions, and it does so on purpose. It lives in its own app — iOS, Android, and on claude.ai — rather than beside Chat, and it is designed around Claude Opus 5.5. It is included in Pro and Max rather than sold separately. The every-morning brief is the product's front door: you wake up and there is a summary of what moved in the accounts you connected and what looks like it needs a decision.

Where Cowork lets you define a schedule, Duet's schedule is partly its own. It checks connected accounts every few hours on a cadence you did not set, which is the whole point and also the whole risk. A system that decides what is urgent without you will sometimes be wrong about urgency. Anthropic says as much in the limitations: Duet might misread a thread or miscalibrate what is urgent, and it is weaker at personal errands than at work tasks. It also cannot yet show you its browser, which is a meaningful gap when the thing you are approving is a web action.

That last limitation is the sharpest honest difference between the two. Cowork's side panel exists so you can watch the browsing happen. Duet's browsing happens in a machine you cannot see into yet, which is precisely why its approval gate is shaped the way it is.

Two threat models, two lists of refusals

This is the most interesting part of the comparison, and it is the part a feature table cannot hold. Both products take real actions, and both were designed by the same company, and yet they refuse different things and gate different things. The divergence is a direct consequence of who is driving.

• Cowork gates deletion. The page is blunt: deleting anything needs your approval. It is the irreversible action, so it gets the veto.

• Cowork gates the plan, if you ask it to. Enable permissions settings and Claude shows its plan and waits for approval before anything significant. That is opt-in, not default, which is a defensible choice for a product where you chose the folder.

• Cowork reaches for integrations first. Computer use goes to your connectors and integrations first, falls back to your browser when needed, and only uses your screen as a last resort. Screen access asks permission per application, and computer use is in research preview for Cowork and for Claude Code.

• Duet refuses some actions outright. Securities trading and moving money between accounts are off the table even with your approval. Not gated — refused.

• Duet refuses some data entirely. A new classifier declines to carry credit card numbers and government identification at all. Health and personal finance information is handled only when you have explicitly instructed it.

Read those two lists side by side and a pattern appears. Cowork's refusals are about reversibility: you are driving, so the system stops at the destructive step and otherwise trusts your scope. Duet's refusals are about blast radius: nobody is driving, so the system declines entire categories of action and data before the question of approval even arises. Pull can afford a per-action gate because you are present for the actions. Push has to draw hard lines in advance because it is acting on a schedule you did not set.

Both cost the same thing, for opposite reasons

Anthropic states the Cowork economics plainly: "Claude Cowork consumes limits faster than Chat," because agentic tasks coordinate multiple sub-agents and tool calls. Duet's announcement states the other half: background checks count toward the same limits, and that is why the checks run every few hours rather than continuously. Those two sentences describe one economic reality approached from two directions. Agentic work is expensive whether a human kicks it off or a timer does, and the plan you are on is the same plan either way.

So the budget question is not "which one is cheaper" — both draw on the same pool. It is "which one spends the pool on work you actually wanted." A Cowork task that you specified is spend you authorised. A Duet check that finds nothing is spend you authorised when you connected the account, and only then. If your month runs short, the honest question is which of the two is earning its share.

The banner you should not read past

Both product pages carry the same piece of news at the top: "Claude Cowork is now just Claude. Rolling out to Pro and Max, with more plans to follow." Anthropic's navigation still lists Claude, Claude Code, Claude Cowork, Claude Duet, and Claude Tag as separate entries, so the surfaces are being reshuffled rather than retired, and the naming has already moved once in public.

The practical consequence is that you should standardise on behaviour, not on a label. The durable questions are: what can this thing reach, when does it stop and ask, and what will it refuse even if I insist. Those answers survive a rename. A team that wrote its policy around "Cowork is where the files work happens" will still be right after the banner changes again; a team that wrote its policy around a menu position will have to rewrite it.

Where the model itself fits

One thing neither surface gives you is the model on its own terms. Claude Duet and Claude Cowork are Anthropic products on Anthropic's plans, and you cannot re-point either of them at a different endpoint — the agent layer and the account are the product. If what you actually need is Claude Opus 5.5 behind your own pipeline, that is a different purchase, and it is the one we sell: 4.00 dollars per million input tokens, 20.00 per million output tokens, a 1M-token context window, and 0.20 dollars per million cache reads, at 0 percent markup, through one OpenAI-compatible key that reaches more than 200 models.

That is a genuinely different job from opening an agent. Duet brings you work it decided on; Cowork completes work you scoped; the API hands you the model and gets out of the way, and everything about initiative, approval, and refusal becomes your code's responsibility rather than Anthropic's interface. Worth being clear about which of those three you are actually buying before you compare a subscription line to a token line.

If you are choosing between the two agents, the question is not which one is better. It is whether this week's work is a set of tasks you already know about, in which case you pull, or a set of things you would not have thought to check, in which case you let something push. Most people will end up using both, and the useful discipline is knowing which mode you are in before you open either window.
