Is the government really on an ‘out-of-control’ spending splurge? It’s complicated

Defence, the NDIS and health responsible for much of increased government outlays and treasurer Jim Chalmers has few levers to pull

“Will the prime minister acknowledge reality … and admit Labor’s out-of-control spending is forcing families and small businesses to pay more for everything?”

That was the opposition leader, Angus Taylor, kicking off parliamentary question time on Wednesday.

Official data earlier that day showed inflation jumped to 4.3% in the year to September, from 4% the month before. Rapid petrol and diesel price increases drove that high headline figure.

But, even removing the impact of soaring fuel prices, underlying inflation ticked higher to 3.7% by the quarterly measure.

Both measures are way above the Reserve Bank’s 2.5% target – and a fourth interest rate hike on Tuesday looks like a done deal.

Frustrated Australians are increasingly inclined to believe Taylor’s argument.

So how much has government spending been rising? Should we blame climbing public expenditure for the cost of living crisis? And what should the government do about it?

Is the government spending more?

This is not surprising as you would expect the government to grow larger alongside the economy and the population.

But, leaving out the extraordinary payments associated with the Covid-19 pandemic, spending has been climbing at an unusually fast pace.

Total government expenses have jumped by 30% between 2021-22 and 2025-26, according to the parliamentary budget office.

Even after accounting for inflation, federal spending increased by 5.5% in 2024-25, and by 4.3% in 2025-26.

Stephen Bartos, a University of Canberra professor and a former deputy secretary at the finance department, says there are three key drivers of growing public outlays.

The first is defence, amid pressure on governments around the world to respond to an increasingly fraught geopolitical reality.

The second is the national disability insurance scheme (NDIS). Bartos says it would be a “herculean task” for the government to successfully halve growth in the scheme to 6% a year.

“That is still twice as fast as the economy is growing,” he says.

“It ratcheted up considerably through Covid but never really came down.”

A more generous pharmaceutical benefits scheme and budgeting for new hospital funding deals with the states were part of the continuing rise in health spending.

Meanwhile, spending on the care economy beyond the NDIS was also climbing rapidly, as was paying interest on our expanding national debt.

Public expenditure as a share of the economy is now at its highest since the early 1980s – leaving out the pandemic period.

But Stephen Smith, a partner at Deloitte Access Economics, argues rising spending would have happened regardless of who held government.

“To be fair, a lot of it [the extra spending over recent years] is structural,” Smith said.

“The world is a complicated place and the Australian economy is more difficult to manage today. There are calls on the government to play a bigger role in the economy, like in the care sectors, that weren’t there in the past.

“But that doesn’t offer the government a blank cheque to spend whatever. They need to run a tight fiscal strategy and make difficult decisions.”

So is all this extra spending adding to inflation?

Smith says it’s not about the level of spending, but the budget position. If you are high spending and equally high taxing, then the net impact could be neutral.

“The easiest way to think about it is if you are running a budget deficit, then you are adding demand into the economy and your fiscal policy is stimulatory. If you are running a surplus, you are taking demand out,” he says.

By that rule of thumb, the government looks culpable.

For all Jim Chalmers’ bluster about hundreds of billions of dollars in savings and better-than-expected bottom lines, the budget papers show a deficit just shy of $10bn in 2024-25, $22.3bn in 2025-26, and an expected shortfall of $31.5bn this financial year.
