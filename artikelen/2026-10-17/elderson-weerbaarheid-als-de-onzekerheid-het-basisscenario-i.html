Ensuring resilience in the face of uncertainty

It is a pleasure to open this joint ECB-Bank of England conference.

Bringing together experts across disciplines from 18 countries and three continents to discuss how we can ensure resilience in an increasingly non-linear world, this event could hardly be more timely.

For much of modern finance, risk management has rested on a simple but consequential assumption: with enough data, sufficient information about borrower behaviour, robust models and a solid understanding of historical patterns, banks can estimate the losses they may face with a reasonable degree of precision.

These estimates inform how banks price risk, set aside provisions for expected losses and determine how much capital they need to absorb any large, unexpected losses. This approach works well when the underlying relationships are broadly stable and has long served as a useful basis for managing financial risks. And of course, today’s modelling is already supplemented by additional layers of risk management, including model overlays, stress testing, underwriting, risk appetite setting and other processes.

But the world facing us today is becoming more complex, more interconnected and more uncertain almost by the day.

Think about geopolitical fragmentation, demographic change, artificial intelligence and quantum computing and, of course, the central theme of this conference: the risks from the ongoing climate and nature (C&N) crises.

These macro trends differ in many respects.

Yet they all share a common denominator: they arise in complex and highly interconnected systems, which often exhibit non-linear behaviour. This means that small changes can spread and trigger disproportionately large effects. These macro trends can amplify shocks through feedback loops. They can lead to abrupt transitions through tipping points. Events that were considered remote tail risks yesterday are moving closer to tomorrow’s baseline.

Addressing non-linearities is undoubtedly challenging but still manageable as long as we remain in the realm of what is measurable. However, an even bigger challenge arises when we enter a world characterised by what Frank Knight termed uncertainty, as distinct from risk, or what John Kay and Mervyn King later popularised as radical uncertainty.[1] The underlying idea is the same: a form of uncertainty in which future events, their probabilities and even the relevant outcomes cannot be measured with any meaningful degree of confidence.

Today I would like to use a simple framework to explore this challenge. The distinction between what we know, what we know we don’t know, and what we don’t even know we don’t know. Or put differently: known knowns, known unknowns and unknown unknowns.

The world we know: the known knowns of C&N risks

Let me begin with what we already know about C&N risks.

Over the past decade, banks and supervisors have made substantial progress in identifying, quantifying and managing C&N risks.[2]

Together, we have built the foundational architecture needed to capture the known knowns of C&N risks in risk management, stress testing and scenario analysis.[3]

Today, we not only see and feel the effects of rising temperatures; increasingly, we can measure them. Thanks to better tools, better data and better modelling, we are increasingly able to quantify the direct damage caused to the economy by C&N risks.[4] Just think about the heavy toll this summers’ extreme weather took on economic activity, labour productivity[5] and supply chains.[6]

Importantly, we also increasingly understand the transmission channels to the financial risk categories facing banks.

For example, with better climate hazard and geo-location data, banks can better model the financial impacts of droughts and flooding on traditional financial risk metrics such as loss given default (LGD).

We know banks’ initial estimate of the impact of different transition scenarios on their capital position.

And we also know that since 2022 the average share of interest income earned from carbon-intensive sectors by significant supervised banks has remained consistently above 70%.

We know the average energy performance of the properties they finance. We know what share of exposures they have in areas vulnerable to physical risk and what proportion of those exposures, in turn, has deteriorating credit quality. While many of these impacts still rely on estimates, banks have made significant strides in recent years, thanks also to the availability of public data sources, which cover an increasing number of physical risk hazards.[7]

But as important as this progress is, it only tells part of the story.[8]

We know that many of the C&N risks that matter most are, by their very nature, forward-looking. And we know that because these risks are very different from past events, they are not fully understood and remain difficult to quantify.

The progress made so far therefore provides a springboard for a more difficult, but necessary conversation: a conversation about some of the fundamental challenges of these risks and uncertainty, a conversation not only about what we know, but also about what we know we do not yet know.

What remains uncertain: the known unknowns of C&N risks

And that brings me to the second category in our framework: the known unknowns.

These are risks that we know exist, but whose timing, magnitude or probabilities remain uncertain.

This is not a new challenge. Economic forecasting operates in a similar environment. The economy, like the climate, is a complex adaptive system. We cannot forecast it with precision, but we can explore plausible scenarios and assess our resilience against them.

We know that tipping points exist but we do not know exactly when they will be reached. Worryingly, scientists have identified several components of the Earth system for which the tipping point may already be approaching, including the dieback of the Amazon rainforest and the collapse of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation.

We also know that compound events can amplify impact. We do not know precisely how such interactions will unfold. A severe drought will likely occur alongside a heatwave. Water scarcity could coincide with a major agricultural disruption. Physical damage could occur simultaneously with pressures on energy systems.[9]

And we know that nature degradation can affect economic activity through complex and interconnected channels. We know that humanity is living well beyond most of its planetary boundaries.[10] In fact, seven of the nine planetary boundaries have already been transgressed. What remains uncertain is how these forces may interact, amplify each other and ultimately propagate through the economy and the financial system.
