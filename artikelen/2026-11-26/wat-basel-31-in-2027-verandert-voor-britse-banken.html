What Basel 3.1 will change for UK banks in 2027

After years of consultation, the Prudential Regulation Authority has chosen the implementation date for Basel 3.1 to begin on 1 January 2027 via the soon-to-be updated PRA Rulebook. Gini experts Sol Kyprou and Joe Bullivant explain how this will change the way UK banks must measure their risk-weighted assets, accounting for credit, market, and operational risk, while placing a limit on the capital benefit of models developed internally.

In January 2026 the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) published its final Basel 3.1 rules. The start date has moved three times, from January 2025 to July 2025, then to January 2026 and finally to January 2027. This time, however, the PRA has issued a rulebook rather than a proposal, which puts firms on a different footing from the earlier deferrals.1 UK banks now have a fixed date to work to: 1 January 2027.

From that date, banks calculate their capital requirements differently. Risk-weighted assets (RWA) measure how risky a bank’s lending is: the riskier the lending, the more capital the bank must hold. The revised rules change how RWA are calculated and limit where banks can use their own models, so more lending has to be measured with the regulator’s standard formulas. For most firms that means a substantial body of remediation work.

Where Basel 3.1 comes from

Basel 3.1 begins with the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), the global standard-setter for banking regulation. Its members are central banks and authorities with direct banking supervisory responsibility, drawn from the major financial jurisdictions.

After the 2008 financial crisis, banks using their own internal ratings-based (IRB) models were getting very different risk-weighted assets for similar portfolios. Because capital is set as a percentage of risk-weighted assets, a bank whose models gave lower numbers could hold less capital for the same risks. The Basel Committee set out to reduce that variability and to monitor the results.

How the output floor works

The output floor is the centrepiece of Basel 3.1. It means the risk-weighted assets a bank calculates with its own models can never be lower than 72.5% of the figure the standardised approach would give. It therefore only affects banks that use internal models, chiefly IRB models for credit risk; for a bank on the standardised approach the two figures are the same. In the UK it applies to a banking group as a whole, and separately only to ring-fenced and stand-alone banks.

In practice a bank works out its total risk-weighted assets twice, once with its own models and once with the standardised approach. It then reports the higher of its modelled figure and the floor, which is 72.5% of the standardised figure. The figure below shows one case each way.

Banks will have time to adjust, because the floor is phased in. It starts at 60% on 1 January 2027, rises to 65% in 2028 and 70% in 2029, and reaches the full 72.5% in 2030. This follows the Basel Committee’s own phase-in, which the UK joins at its 60% step.

The result is that banks can only cut their capital so far by using their own models. That affects return on equity and puts more pressure on the development of in-house models.

Which exposures keep internal models

Basel 3.1 keeps the IRB approach, but banks can use it for fewer exposures and its input floors are tighter. Equity exposures lose it entirely and move to the standardised approach, although firms with existing equity IRB permissions have until the end of 2029 to switch.

Other exposure classes keep some IRB options. Exposures to large corporates and financial corporates lose Advanced IRB (A-IRB) but keep Foundation IRB (F-IRB). Exposures to income-producing real estate (IPRE), which can include high-volatility commercial real estate, lose both. These move to the standardised approach, though a bank can apply to use slotting instead, which sorts exposures into categories with risk weights set by the supervisor.

Basel 3.1’s IRB restrictions confine the full Advanced IRB benefit to retail portfolios, other general corporates, and certain specialised lending sub-classes.

What changes for credit, market and operational risk?

The credit risk SA is the most revised part, with risk weights that are more sensitive to borrower type and collateral quality.

Counterparty credit risk changes less: the standardised approach is largely unchanged, and banks can still use the Internal Model Method (IMM) to model exposure on derivatives and securities financing transactions. A guide to counterparty credit risk is available on the Gini website, and sets out the governance behind that capital number. The bigger change is to credit valuation adjustment (CVA) risk, the risk of losses when a counterparty’s credit quality worsens, where banks lose the option to use internal models.

Operational risk moves to a single standardised approach based on the business indicator: a three-year simple average of income and expense components calculated at the firm’s financial year end. The existing set of approaches, including the Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA), is withdrawn.

Moving the CRR into the PRA Rulebook

Basel 3.1 is not the only reason UK capital rules change in 2027. Before Brexit, the Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) sat alongside the PRA Rulebook as a separate but equally binding source of rules. After Brexit the CRR stayed in UK law, amended so that it worked outside the EU. Because it is legislation, the PRA cannot amend it as freely as its own rules. The PRA is therefore moving the CRR’s rules into its Rulebook in stages, timed to land with Basel 3.1 and spare firms a second round of change.

Five policy statements are involved: three move the CRR across, one sets up a simpler regime for small banks, and one revises Pillar 2A.

PS1/26 rewrites many of the same rules under Basel 3.1, and most of it takes effect on the same day, 1 January 2027, with the internal models approach for market risk following in 2028.3 From January 2027 the Rulebook holds the restated requirements, and banks still read it with the supervisory statements, statements of policy and the few definitions that remain in legislation.

What this means for UK banks

The Basel standard was finalised in December 2017, and the PRA has worked on the UK version since 2022. With PS1/26, UK banks now have final rules to work to.

From 1 January 2027, the savings that banks could get from their own capital models are capped, with those models accepted in fewer places. The standardised approaches around them arrive in revised form. And the CRR moves into the PRA Rulebook, so a UK bank’s capital rules are both rewritten and re-homed on the same day. What remains is for UK banks to be ready by 1 January 2027.
