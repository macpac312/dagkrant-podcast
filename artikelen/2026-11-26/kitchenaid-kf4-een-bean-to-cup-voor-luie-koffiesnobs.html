KitchenAid Fully Automatic Espresso Machine KF4 With Iced Coffee

Pros & Cons

I’ve been testing KitchenAid’s bean-to-cup espresso machine for months, and it’s like having a Waymo that’s designed to make lattes, cappuccinos, flat whites and other coffee shop drinks. It takes care of everything all while keeping my kitchen tidy.

Its full name, the KitchenAid Fully Automatic Espresso Machine KF4 With Iced Coffee, doesn’t quite roll off the tongue, so I’ll call it the KF4. While its name doesn’t evoke brevity, its contemporary design does, with a compact footprint that fits on my tiny kitchen counters.

The KF4 is ridiculously easy to use. You add coffee beans, water and milk (for some drinks), then press the picture of the drink you want. You don’t need to grind any beans, measure a dose, tamp any portafilters, knock grinds out or clean the shower screen. It does all of this for you.

All you need to do is whatever the KF4 tells you. It’s like Laddie from The Simpsons and will alert you when it needs you to take care of something, like emptying what might be the largest drip tray I’ve ever seen on a home espresso machine.

At a time when making espresso drinks at home has really taken off, fully automatic machines like the KitchenAid KF4 prioritize convenience and making tasty beverages. But KitchenAid has a lot to prove in order to stand out from a crowded field of automatic machine competitors from brands such as De’Longhi, Miele, Terra Kaffe and Ninja. After nearly half a year with the KF4, I can say it still consistently makes wonderful drinks and has become a staple in the CNET office kitchen.

At $1,299, the KitchenAid KF4 isn’t cheap, but I think it’s worth the price for the convenience and quality if that’s what you’re looking for. Right now, for Black Friday, the KF4 espresso machine is on sale for $1,000 ($300 off).

KitchenAid KF4 espresso machine buying advice

If you’re a Customized Coffee Charlie and like tweaking every aspect of your espresso routine, the KF4 isn’t for you. If you’re a Lazy Latte Laura who wants espresso drinks like the ones you get at a specialty shop and are OK with emptying a drip tray and spent grounds container, this is for you.

The KF4 has been a great fit in our office, as it doesn’t require people to learn how to use a semiautomatic espresso machine with a portafilter and a custom workflow. The machine would be fantastic for a household with many different coffee needs and varying levels of interest and patience in the process.

You get excellent espresso drinks at the touch of a button that rival what you buy at a coffee shop and are tastier than what you get from Keurig or Nespresso.

The KitchenAid KF4 makes excellent espresso

I’ve tried a variety of coffees with the KF4, ranging from natural-process specialty beans to off-the-shelf grocery-store espresso roasts, and the shots were consistently good. Using fresher coffee and adjusting the built-in burr grinder yielded the best taste and texture. Shots from the KF4 look rich, with pronounced body, heart and crema, while lacking the over-aerated crema produced by some automated machines.

I appreciate being able to customize the temperature on espresso and drinks. I can set it higher for lighter roasts, like Hedge Coffee’s Columbia, to bring out more complexity and a deeper flavor, or set it low for Intelligentsia’s Black Cat Classic Espresso blend. Most of the time, I’ve used Equator Coffee’s Dipsea Espresso, a versatile blend perfect for milk drinks and Americanos.

The KF4 pulls single shots of espresso, so if you’re making a milk drink, I’d recommend using two. I like that it will pull two single shots in a row instead of trying to pull a double, which can mess with the brew ratio on other fully automatic machines.

The KF4 offers a menu of classic espresso drinks but is oddly missing options for an espresso macchiato and cortado, the latter being my usual go-to. Drinks can be customized, so to get something close to a cortado, I select a flat white with two shots and then reduce the amount of milk to its lowest setting.

It can steam both cow and alternative milks, but the texture with almond or oat milk wasn’t as rich as it was with dairy. No matter which hot milk drink you make with the KF4, all benefit from a good stir; otherwise, foam tends to sit on top rather than be incorporated. The KF4 knows the difference between a cappuccino and a flat white, but frustratingly, it lacks controls to customize milk foam and texture. This compromise is definitely worth considering. Are you prepared to give up your preferences for how milk is steamed with a $1,300 machine? Rival fully automatic machines, such as the De’Longhi Rivelia, allow adjustment of how milk is steamed.

In terms of taste, milk drinks are great, even if the foam is just OK. I alternate between a flat white and a cappuccino, both of which I make with less milk and two shots. I also like that the milk’s temperature is consistent – yay, automation! I don’t get an overly hot drink one day followed by another that feels like it could have been steamed more.

The machine offers espresso and iced espresso options, which pull a shot at a lower temperature to improve flavor and reduce ice melt. I was skeptical about the iced espresso setting, so I ran a test: I pulled an espresso and an iced espresso, each over a cup with the same amount of ice. I then used a strainer to see how much liquid came out. The regular espresso shot poured over ice yielded 10 grams more liquid than the iced espresso shot – meaning it was much more watered down. By the way, as a barista for 11 years, anytime we made iced drinks, we’d just pour the espresso shot over ice, since the La Marzocco at our cafe lacked an iced espresso option.

To my surprise, iced espresso had a fuller, rounder taste than just a regular shot over ice. As for the machine’s namesake, the iced coffee, it’s really an iced Americano made with the iced espresso setting. It’s good, but it lacks the body and fuller flavor of an iced coffee you might get at a coffee bar or make with a pour-over.

KitchenAid KF4 design and features

At 7.7 inches wide, the KF4 is quite compact, but it’s also 18.5 inches long, so it eats up counter depth. I appreciate the KF4’s minimalist look. There’s an inconspicuous and sophisticated air to the machine that I appreciate. When you power it on, a ring of light around the spout head gives off some serious Tron vibes.

The hopper isn’t visible and sits under a top panel lid with a silicone seal to keep air out — extra points for not being see-through, which helps prevent light damage to the beans. The hopper isn’t removable, so you need to use any beans in it before adding more. In our office, we all have different coffee tastes, and since switching out the beans isn’t possible, we appreciate the ground coffee chute bypass, especially for those wanting an afternoon decaf espresso.

Grind control is located under the machine’s removable side panel. These adjustments really work, which is not always the case with all automatic machines. In fact, you can see the difference between two pucks I broke apart below, one from when I had the grinder set coarser and the other finer.

The 1.8-liter (60-ounce) water tank is a great size. It’s easy to take off, fill and put right back in place. I don’t have to fiddle with it to get it to slide back onto the machine.

The adjustable spout, which dispenses espresso and steamed milk, slides up and down with ease for different cup sizes, and it stays in place. A removable tube connects the spout head to the milk container (or the drip tray for cleaning). Many fully automatic machines use a similar design. The milk container is made of BPA-free plastic, but it scratches easily. By contrast, some competing espresso makers have glass milk containers or use a stainless steel pitcher. KitchenAid charges $60 for an additional milk container.

Every time you power the machine on, it runs a quick clean cycle and flush. I imagine this is in part to heat it up. Espresso takes about 20-30 seconds to pull and drinks like a latte can take upwards of a minute, depending on how you have settings customized. If I were making drinks that fast as a barista on a manual machine, I’d have deserved quite the raise. While I’m fond of manual and semiautomatic espresso machines, like my refurbished and accessorized Breville Bambino Plus, using the KF4 has been a breath of fresh air for workflow and cleanliness.

It’s also quite serious about preventing limescale, the silent killer of home brewers. The first time I powered on the KF4, it asked me to test the water, thus starting our relationship off on the right foot. I was flattered that it cared to know the hardness of the water I was using. The machine comes with a water testing kit, and part of the setup involves entering the result into it. The water tank has its own water filter and when you start the machine, it runs a quick clean and rinse cycle.

The KitchenAid KF4 is perhaps one of the cleanest espresso machines that I’ve ever tested. Making coffee at home can be messy. If you’re pulling espresso and frothing milk, that mess can mean that you spend as much time cleaning as you do sipping on the flat white you made.

You can add profiles and each can have its own drink favorites. We didn’t use this feature really at the office, but I could see it being clutch for a family or a household.

Final thoughts on the KitchenAid KF4

Fully automatic machines, like KitchenAid’s KF4, offer a mix of convenience and delicious espresso drinks. But with the KF4, you give up the ability to customize milk, easily swap out coffee beans for a different roast and you’re going to pay $1,300 – unless you can find it on sale. Clearly, some people have the budget and are willing to accept those compromises to make good espresso beverages at the touch of a button. And for those folks, the KitchenAid could be a good fit. For me, I still find myself going to it by default for a quick cortado — I mean, a customized flat white.
